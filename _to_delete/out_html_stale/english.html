P1 Before We Read: Sounds, Dreams, and Jahnavi40 min
  • Close-eyes listening: sounds around us
  • Jahnavi's dream: predict and share
  • After Section I: Jahnavi's dream and the River
  • Teacher opens with the pre-reading activity: students close their eyes for a minute and listen to the sounds around them, then share with classmates and the teacher whether the sounds seem to carry a message (page 1). Pair-sharing is encouraged before whole-class discussion.
  • Teacher introduces Jahnavi — a bright girl in a coastal village — and poses the open prediction task: what could her dream be, and how might she make it come true (page 1)? Students share responses before the teacher begins reading Section I of the prose aloud.
  • Students read Section I independently, then discuss the two questions on Jahnavi's dream and whether the River can help her fulfil it (page 3). Teacher draws out the River's role as a gentle, personified counsellor.
  • Teacher consolidates by asking students to hold one 'wondering question' Jahnavi might ask the River, bridging to Section II in the next unit.
Teacher notes: This unit sets the emotional core of the story — Jahnavi's exclusion from school and her longing to learn. Keep pre-reading oral activities brisk (8 minutes each) so Section I reading and discussion receive adequate time. Note that the detailed nature descriptions at the story's opening (kingfisher, lizard, river murmur) are worth a brief mention as the writer's way of immersing the reader in Jahnavi's world.
P2 Jahnavi and the River: Questions, Advice, and Courage40 min
  • Table: Jahnavi's questions and River's answers
  • Extract 1 analysis: River's tone and Jahnavi's character
  • Brief recap of Unit 1: teacher asks two students to recall Jahnavi's main worry and the River's first response. This transitions the class into Section II of the prose.
  • Students read Section II and then complete the matching table — Jahnavi's curious questions in Column 1 and the River's answers in Column 2 (page 6). Teacher circulates and checks pairs before class-wide correction.
  • Class reads the extract 'You shouldn't cry, you know…I've got to hurry to reach the sea, you know' and answers the four questions on the River's tone, Jahnavi's repeated visits, the exclamation mark's effect, and the River's qualities (page 7). Teacher facilitates brief responses.
  • Teacher sets homework: complete the four questions on Extract 2 ('Can I do something?…They'd chase me out') at home (pages 7–8) and previews that the next unit will examine Jahnavi's triumph.
Teacher notes: The table task anchors students in the River's wit — especially the contrast between Jahnavi's wonder-questions about nature and the River's practical advice about going to school. In Extract 1, guide students to notice that the River is given a capital letter throughout the story, hinting at the personification concept that surfaces in the Vocabulary/Grammar spine. Extract 2 questions are moved to homework as time does not permit a third extract in this unit.
Homework: Extract 2 questions on River's advice (page 7–8)

  • Match statements 1–7 to speakers (i)–(v)

P4 Sound Words, Prepositions, and the River's Capital60 min
  • Personification: why 'River' has a capital letter
  • Sound-word matching and fill-in-the-blanks
  • Fill blanks with prepositions in Jahnavi's school day
  • Distinguish preposition vs adverb in eight sentences
  • Teacher leads Task I: why does the author capitalise 'River' when it is a common noun? Students discuss the clue (River given human qualities) and record a one-sentence answer (page 8). Teacher confirms the personification device.
  • Task II: students pick sound words from the text and match them to nine definitions (page 8), then fill the six blanks in the classroom scene using the matched sound words (page 9). Teacher corrects the matching on the board before students attempt the fill-in.
  • Task VI: students fill six blanks with prepositions from the brackets as Jahnavi walks through her first school day (page 11). Teacher explains each choice in context — 'at school' (location), 'outside the gate' (position), 'through the classroom' (movement direction).
  • Task VII: teacher explains that words like on, off, up, down can serve as either preposition or adverb depending on whether they link to a noun. Students complete eight sentences (pages 11–12) and two volunteers share answers for class discussion.
  • Quick consolidation: teacher asks students to give one example of a sound word and one of a preposition used in the day's tasks, then previews the writing period.
Teacher notes: Task I links naturally to the extract analysis from Unit 2 — students already noticed the River speaking — so the personification explanation should take no more than ten minutes. Tasks III (borrowed words) and IV–V (boats, crossword) are rich but time-consuming and are best left as self-study or home exploration; flag them briefly as optional extension. The preposition versus adverb distinction in Task VII is the hardest concept — use board examples before releasing students to work independently.

P6 King Bruce and the Spider: First Reading and Summary40 min
  • Pre-reading: who inspires you to do your best?
  • Fill-in summary of the poem using box words
  • Complete sentences: narrative style and central idea
  • Pre-reading warm-up: students share who or what inspires them to do their best (page 16). Teacher takes three or four responses and links the idea of unlikely sources of inspiration to the poem's spider.
  • Teacher reads the poem aloud with expression, emphasising the mounting tension as the spider attempts climb after climb. Students follow in their textbooks, noting the ABAB rhyme scheme and the repeated phrases 'tried and tried', 'up, up', 'steadily, steadily'.
  • Students complete the fill-in summary (page 19) using the eight box words (tried, mounted, despair, attempts, great, conquered, low, strive), then complete the two sentence-completion items on narrative style and central idea (page 19). Teacher corrects on the board.
  • Teacher introduces the term 'extended metaphor' briefly — the spider's repeated climbs mirror Bruce's own repeated failures — and previews that Unit 7 will examine the poem's literary devices in depth.
Teacher notes: Read the poem at a measured pace; the cumulative tension of the spider's nine attempts is best felt when the teacher slows slightly at each fall and picks up pace on each new ascent. The fill-in summary anchors comprehension before literary analysis begins. Pre-reading tasks II (pictures of nature) and the later extract tasks are not anchored in this unit; flag extract analysis as the focus for Unit 7.
P7 Repetition, Metaphor, and the Spider's Defiance of Despair40 min
  • Pick examples: spider's efforts and alliteration
  • Why does the poet repeat 'tried and tried'; 'steadily, steadily'; 'up, up'?
  • Identify metaphors and similes in nine sentences
  • Students pick three examples from the poem for lines describing the spider's efforts and three examples of alliteration (page 19). Pairs compare choices before a brief class share.
  • Teacher leads discussion on why the poet repeats 'tried and tried', 'steadily, steadily', and 'up, up' (page 19). Students write an extended answer; teacher guides them to see that repetition enacts — not merely describes — the persistence it celebrates.
  • Teacher introduces metaphor vs simile using the poem's spider-as-Bruce comparison as the anchor, then students read nine sentences and label each M or S (page 20). Teacher corrects on the board and asks students to justify two choices.
  • Homework assigned: complete the three fill-in MCQ items on rhyme scheme, 'Bravo' as interjection, and 'twas/'tis for rhythm (page 19). Teacher previews that Unit 8 will cover listening, speaking and the letter-writing task.
Teacher notes: The repetition question is the conceptual centrepiece of this unit — guide students beyond 'the poet repeats for emphasis' to the richer idea that the rhythm of repetition mirrors the spider's physical rhythm of climb and fall. Task VI.2 (transforming similes to metaphors) is omitted from class due to time; it can be assigned as extension homework for stronger students. Extract I and II close-reading questions are also left as self-study; flag them in teacher notes.
Homework: Fill in three MCQ items on rhyme scheme and poetic devices (page 19)

P8 The Persistent Ant: Listening and Personal Narration40 min
  • Predict then verify: five MCQs on the ant story
  • Narrate a personal perseverance experience using prompts
  • Students read the five MCQ questions about the ant story silently and make predictions before listening (page 24). Teacher asks two students to share their predictions and reasoning.
  • Teacher reads/plays the ant story (transcript p.40) at natural pace. Students listen and check their predicted answers, then verify as a class. Teacher briefly draws the parallel between the ant's determination and the spider's in the poem.
  • Speaking task: students narrate a personal experience of perseverance using the seven-step prompt framework — Introduction, Describe the challenge, Feeling disheartened, The source of motivation, Taking action, The result, Reflection (page 25). Three or four students present; peers listen actively.
  • Teacher gives brief oral feedback on two narrations — noting strong use of transition language and vivid detail — and previews that Unit 9 will cover vocabulary, grammar, and writing for Section B.
Teacher notes: The prediction step before listening is important: it activates prior knowledge and gives students a purpose for listening. For the speaking task, ensure every student who speaks uses at least three of the seven prompt steps; the 'Source of motivation' step is most directly linked to the poem's theme. Listening Task II (re-listen and verify) is folded into the class correction after the first play to save time.

P9 Antonyms, Distance Words, and a Letter of Perseverance50 min
  • Write opposites of five poem words
  • Classify distance words as far or near
  • Write letter to cousin on overcoming difficulty, using transition words
  • Students write the opposites of five words drawn from the poem — up, fast, glad, win, succeed (page 22). Teacher corrects quickly and then asks students to locate two of these words in the poem to see them in their emotional context.
  • Teacher explains the poem's distance language ('a long way up', 'inch by inch', 'half-yard higher') before students classify the box words and phrases as far or near (page 23). Teacher corrects on the board and briefly explains 'proximity', 'vicinity', 'yonder', and 'afar'.
  • Writing task: teacher displays the letter scaffold on the board (opening, three body paragraphs with transition words, closing) and students write a letter to their cousin on how to overcome a difficult situation, using 'To begin with', 'Next', 'After that', 'Then', 'Finally' as structural markers (page 26). Teacher circulates and gives formative feedback on format and transition word use.
  • Teacher asks one volunteer to read their opening paragraph aloud and gives one targeted compliment and one improvement suggestion before transitioning to Section C in Unit 10.
Teacher notes: Vocabulary Tasks I and III are kept short to protect the writing time — the letter is the unit's main output and requires at least 28 minutes for a draft of reasonable quality. Task II (word meanings and own sentences for endeavour, toil, strive, braced) and Task IV (tongue twisters) are good self-study candidates; mention them briefly as extension. Remind students that the letter format — date, greeting, transition words, closing — is a functional writing skill they will use across subjects.

P10 Helen Keller's Three Days: Reading and Comprehension40 min
  • Identify true statements from seven on Keller's essay
  • Table: what Keller planned for each of the three days
  • Teacher briefly introduces Helen Keller — blind and deaf author who accessed the world entirely through touch, smell, and sound — and reads the essay's opening paragraph aloud to establish tone. Students follow in their textbooks.
  • Students read the full essay silently, then work in pairs to identify the true statements from the seven given (page 31), distinguishing carefully between what Keller actually says and plausible but incorrect options. Teacher facilitates class correction.
  • Students complete the three-column table of what Keller plans to do on Day 1 (see the people she loves), Day 2 (arise with the dawn, visit museums), and Day 3 (observe a busy city corner) (page 32). Teacher models the Day 1 entry on the board before students complete Days 2 and 3.
  • Homework: students write a short answer to 'How is the narrator able to understand the feelings of a person?' (page 32) based on Keller's description of knowing friends only through the feel of their faces. Unit 11 will cover vocabulary, speaking, and the writing task.
Teacher notes: Keller's essay moves from quiet wonder to passionate advocacy — a tone shift that students in the middle stage may need help tracking. The true/false-style identification task is a good comprehension check before the three-day table, which requires students to synthesise across the full essay. The pre-reading activities (pair recall of a visited place and senses discussion, pages 28–29) are left as self-study since the unit is fully used by the comprehension and table tasks.
Homework: How does the narrator understand a person's feelings? (page 32)
spineVocabulary Grammar + Speaking
P11 Modal Verbs, Sensory Words, and Speaking About the Senses40 min
  • Identify modal verbs and their functions in five sentences
  • Fill blanks with appropriate modal verbs in seven situations
  • Speak one minute on the sense you value most
  • Teacher introduces modal verbs using Keller's own sentences ('I should arise with the dawn', 'I must spend the second day') and explains the five functions: possibility, necessity, polite request, ability, moral obligation. Students identify modal verbs and their functions in the five given sentences (page 34).
  • Students fill the seven blanks with appropriate modal verbs matching the given function labels — permission, obligation, advice, possibility, polite request, moral obligation, suggestion (page 35). Teacher corrects on the board, emphasising why 'ought to' signals moral obligation while 'should' signals advice.
  • Speaking task: students prepare for one minute, then two or three volunteers speak for one minute on the sense they value most, using the prompt framework from page 36. Class listens and identifies one strong phrase from each speaker.
  • Teacher closes the chapter by connecting Keller's appeal — 'use your eyes as if tomorrow you might be struck blind' — to the chapter's overarching theme of valuing what we have and learning with gratitude. Sensory-word task (Task I, page 34) and beyond-text tasks (Braille, ISL, IDPD) are flagged as self-study extension for interested students.
Teacher notes: Modal verbs are the hardest grammar point in Section C — students often confuse 'can' (ability) with 'may' (permission) and 'should' (advice) with 'must' (necessity). Use Keller's repeated 'should' to demonstrate hypothetical modal use before moving to the fill-in task. The speaking activity here (public-speaking method) connects directly to Keller's essay: students practise the same grateful-advocacy voice that Keller models. Writing Task (descriptive paragraph, page 37) is not anchored in class; flag it as a homework or follow-up assignment.
