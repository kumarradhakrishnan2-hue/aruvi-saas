Lesson Plan — Exploring Substances: Acidic, Basic, and Neutral (science, grade Grade VII, 7 periods)

progression_stageObserve and sort by indicator response
implied_lo Students can sort a set of household substances into acidic, basic, and neutral groups by observing colour changes in blue litmus, red litmus, and red rose extract, and can state which colour change corresponds to which group.stage_number 1description Students encounter the phenomenon of colour change in natural indicators and sort familiar substances into acidic, basic, and neutral groups using litmus and rose extract.
P1 What Does Litmus Tell Us?40 min
  • Students test lemon juice, soap solution, amla juice, tamarind water, vinegar, baking soda solution, lime water, tap water, washing powder solution, sugar solution, and salt solution with both blue and red litmus papers, recording colour changes and sorting each substance into Group A (acidic), Group B (basic), or Group C (neutral).
  • The science fair story of Ashwin and Keerthi is read aloud; the question of how to tell substances apart using natural materials is posed to anchor the activity.
  • The correct technique for litmus paper testing is demonstrated, and the observation table structure is explained so groups know exactly what to record.
  • Groups systematically test all eleven household samples with blue then red litmus paper, recording colour changes and tentative groupings in their tables.
  • Groups share their Group A, B, and C lists with the class; patterns across groups are noted and any disagreements are discussed to build a shared reference table.
Teacher notes: Open by letting students predict before they test — the surprise when a familiar food turns litmus an unexpected colour is what drives the sorting. Watch for the assumption that 'sour' always means acidic and 'bitter' always basic; tap water and sugar solution are the useful neutral counter-examples here. Keep the eleven samples labelled and in the same order across the table so the next unit can reuse them directly.
P2 Rose Petals as a Colour Signal40 min
  • Students prepare a red rose petal extract by crushing petals and filtering, then test the same eleven household samples from Unit 1, observing whether the extract turns red (acidic) or green (basic) or remains unchanged (neutral), and compare these results with their litmus groupings.
  • Students predict which colour they expect the rose extract to show for each group before testing, recording predictions alongside the Unit 1 reference table.
  • Groups prepare red rose petal extract by crushing petals in a small amount of water and filtering through cloth or filter paper into a collection vessel.
  • Groups add a few drops of rose extract to each of the eleven samples and record the observed colour in a new column of their observation table.
  • Groups compare their rose extract groupings with their litmus groupings and report whether the two methods agree, identifying any discrepancies for class discussion.
Teacher notes: This unit reuses Unit 1's eleven samples, so students are now comparing a second indicator against results they already trust. The common slip is treating the rose-petal extract as a brand-new test rather than asking whether it agrees with litmus — push them to line the two columns up. If an extract gives a weak or ambiguous colour, that is a real observation about indicator sensitivity, not an error to hide.
progression_stageCompare indicators and classify by chemical property
implied_lo Students can explain the limitation of turmeric paper as an indicator, compare the response patterns of all three indicators for the same set of substances, and identify physical properties (sour taste, soapy feel) that correlate with acidic and basic classification.stage_number 2description Students test substances with turmeric paper and olfactory indicators, compare results across all three indicator types, and articulate the chemical properties (taste, feel, colour change pattern) that define each group.
P3 Turmeric Paper and Its Limits40 min
  • Students prepare turmeric paper strips by soaking filter paper in turmeric paste and drying, then test the same sample set, observing that only basic substances turn the yellow paper red while acidic and neutral substances both leave it unchanged, identifying this limitation and comparing all three indicator response patterns.
  • Groups prepare turmeric paper by applying turmeric paste to filter paper strips and allowing them to dry briefly, while discussing what colour change would indicate a basic substance.
  • Groups test each of the eleven samples with dry turmeric paper strips and record whether the paper changes colour or remains yellow.
  • Groups add a turmeric paper column to their master observation table and compare responses across all three indicators, identifying which indicator can distinguish acidic from neutral and which cannot.
  • Groups state in one sentence what turmeric paper can and cannot tell us, and share these statements to build a class consensus on indicator limitations.
Teacher notes: Having met two natural indicators, students now meet one that only responds to bases — turmeric paper. The frequent misconception is that every indicator must react to both acids and bases; turmeric's silence on acids is the whole point, so resist the urge to call a non-reaction a failed test. Let groups discover the limit themselves before you name it.
P4 Smell, Taste, and Touch — Chemical Properties of Each Group40 min
  • Students investigate olfactory indicators using onion-soaked strips alongside Activity 2.2's guided taste and touch observations, linking the physical properties of sourness, bitterness, and slipperiness to their acidic and basic classifications, and discover why indicators are needed when taste and touch are unsafe for unknown substances.
  • Students revisit their classification table and, for safe identified substances only, record taste (sour/bitter/neutral) and touch (soapy/slippery/none) observations alongside their indicator groupings.
  • Students test onion-soaked paper strips against safe acidic and basic samples, observing whether the characteristic onion odour diminishes or persists, and record results.
  • Groups examine their completed table and identify the pattern: acidic substances are sour-tasting; basic substances are soapy, slippery, and bitter; and why neither taste nor touch is safe for unknown substances.
  • Each group shares one property-to-group link they discovered; the class constructs a shared property summary for acidic, basic, and neutral groups to be used as reference in subsequent units.
Teacher notes: Build on the indicator work by shifting attention from colour to the substances' own physical properties — smell, and the cautious taste/touch observations from Activity 2.2. Reinforce safety: tasting and touching are only ever done with the teacher-approved samples, never generalised to unknowns. The link to watch is students connecting a property (slippery, sour) back to the acid/base group they already established.
progression_stageExplain neutralisation as a chemical change and apply to real contexts
implied_lo Students can explain why mixing an acid and a base in sufficient quantities produces a neutral solution, identify the products of neutralisation (salt, water, heat), and justify the selection of a corrective substance for a given real-world acidic or basic problem using both classification and neutralisation reasoning.stage_number 3description Students reason about what happens when an acid meets a base, identify the products of a neutralisation reaction, and apply this causal mechanism to explain and evaluate real-world scenarios involving soil correction, ant bites, and industrial waste — integrating both classification and chemical change reasoning simultaneously.
P5 Acid Meets Base — What Changes?40 min
  • Students investigate what happens when lemon juice is progressively added to lime water containing blue litmus solution, observing the transition from blue to red as an indicator of chemical change, inferring that the acidic and basic properties have been cancelled out, and constructing the neutralisation equation with teacher guidance.
  • Students predict what they think will happen when lemon juice (acidic) is added to lime water (basic) containing blue litmus solution, writing their predictions before any demonstration.
  • Groups add lemon juice drop by drop to lime water with blue litmus solution, recording the colour of the solution after each addition until the solution turns red, noting the point of apparent neutrality.
  • Groups compare their observations with their predictions and discuss what must have happened to the acidic and basic properties, constructing an explanation using their own words before formal vocabulary is introduced.
  • The neutralisation reaction is formalised: Acid + Base → Salt + Water + Heat; students write this equation in their notes alongside their observation record and identify what each product means.
Teacher notes: This is the pivot from sorting to explaining — adding lemon juice drop by drop to lime water lets students see a base become neutral and then acidic in real time. The classic error is reading the colour change as the acid 'disappearing' rather than the two reacting; ask what happened to the base as the colour shifted. Going slowly near the colour-change point is what makes neutralisation visible.
P6 Neutralisation at Work — Three Situations40 min
  • Students analyse the three real-world situations from Section 2.3 — ant bite treated with baking soda, acidic or basic soil corrected with lime or compost, and acidic factory waste neutralised before lake release — identifying in each case the problem substance, the corrective substance, and the neutralisation reaction that relieves the problem.
  • Situation 1 (ant bite and formic acid) is presented; students identify that the skin substance is acidic, baking soda is basic, and explain how applying a base neutralises the acid to relieve pain.
  • Situation 2 (soil correction) is presented in both acidic and basic variants; students identify what lime does to acidic soil and what compost does to basic soil, explaining both directions of neutralisation.
  • Situation 3 (factory waste and lake) is presented; students explain why the factory must treat acidic waste with a basic substance before release and what harm is avoided.
  • Students compare all three situations and identify the common pattern: in every case, a substance of opposite nature is selected to neutralise the problem, producing a less harmful result.
Teacher notes: With neutralisation observed, students now reason about why it matters in three real situations from Section 2.3. Watch for answers that name the remedy (baking soda, lime) without naming what it neutralises — push them to state both the problem's nature and the correction. Asking 'acid or base?' for each situation before choosing the fix keeps the reasoning honest.
P7 Solve the Science Fair Mystery — Classify and Neutralise60 min
  • Students receive a set of unfamiliar scenarios requiring them to first identify the nature of each substance using indicator evidence, then select and justify a corrective substance using neutralisation reasoning — culminating in explaining how the chapter's opening science fair mystery (turmeric-coated paper and soap writing) worked.
  • Groups recall their indicator response tables and neutralisation equation from previous units to prepare for the challenge scenarios, identifying what information they will need for each step.
  • Groups work through three unfamiliar scenarios: for each, they identify the nature of the problem substance using indicator reasoning, select a corrective substance, and write a justification referencing both classification evidence and the neutralisation reaction.
  • Groups apply their integrated reasoning to the science fair mystery: they explain which indicator was used (turmeric), what the writing substance was (soap solution), and why the turmeric paper turned red — linking this back to basic substance detection and their classification work.
  • Groups share their science fair mystery explanations with the class; key reasoning steps are consolidated, and any remaining gaps between classification and neutralisation reasoning are addressed as a whole group.
Teacher notes: This closing unit asks students to classify unfamiliar substances and then choose a neutralising agent with justification — the full arc in one task. The common shortcut is guessing the substance's nature from its name instead of from indicator evidence; insist they cite the test result first. Let groups defend their reasoning aloud, since the justification is what you are really assessing.

MCQ In a neutralisation experiment, lemon juice is added drop by drop to lime water containing blue litmus solution. Initially the solution is blue. After enough lemon juice is added, the solution turns red. Which statement best explains what this colour change indicates?
  1. The lemon juice has diluted the lime water so much that the indicator has faded.
  2. The basic property of lime water has been overcome by the acid in lemon juice, producing a solution that is now acidic.
  3. The acid and base have reacted completely; the resulting solution is neutral and contains salt and water.
  4. The acid in lemon juice has reacted with the base in lime water; the excess acid after neutralisation makes the solution acidic, turning the litmus red.
Answer: D
Implied LO: Students can explain why mixing an acid and a base in sufficient quantities produces a neutral solution, identify the products of neutralisation (salt, water, heat), and justify the selection of a corrective substance for a given real-world acidic or basic problem using both classification and neutralisation reasoning.

The farmer wants both fields to be suitable for farming, which requires nearly neutral soil. Explain, for each field separately: (a) the chemical nature of the soil problem; (b) the corrective substance the farmer should add; (c) how the neutralisation reaction solves the problem, naming the products formed.

Teacher guide:
  • Field 1 is correctly identified as basic (red litmus turns blue) and Field 2 as acidic (blue litmus turns red).
  • Correct corrective substance for Field 1 (basic soil): organic matter or compost, which releases acids to neutralise the base.
  • Correct corrective substance for Field 2 (acidic soil): lime (a base), to neutralise the excess acid.
  • The neutralisation reaction is written or described as: Acid + Base → Salt + Water + Heat for each field's correction.
  • The explanation connects the chemical change to the practical outcome: the soil becomes neither acidic nor basic, making it suitable for farming.
  • Both fields are addressed separately with distinct reasoning, not a single generic statement.
