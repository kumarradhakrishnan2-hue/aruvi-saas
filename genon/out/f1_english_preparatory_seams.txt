X−1 (the class's own last lesson)  ·  sitting 7  ·  Float or Sink? Hands-On Water Exploration
anchor: 'B|beyond_text'   synthesis: None

TEACHER NOTE
  The float-and-sink activity grows naturally from the poem's final splash — students have imagined a giant laddoo hitting a giant sea, and now they investigate what actually happens to objects in water. Ensure each group has a shallow tub and a set of objects ready before the sitting begins; the activity window is not long enough to source materials mid-class. Prediction before testing is important — it makes the experiment honest and the surprise real.

MATERIALS
  ['Textbook p.75', 'Tubs of water (one per group)', 'Objects: piece of paper, small stone, green leaf, pencil, eraser, feather, spoon', 'Notebooks']

BAND[0] 0-6
  Teacher connects to the poem's final image — the giant laddoo thrown into the giant sea — and asks: 'What do you think happened? Did the laddoo float or sink?' Students predict briefly; teacher notes that today the class will test real objects in water.

BAND[1] 6-14
  Teacher displays or names each of the seven objects from page 75 (piece of paper, small stone, green leaf, pencil, eraser, feather, spoon). Students look at the textbook pictures and write their prediction (float or sink) for each object before testing.

BAND[2] 14-29
  Let us explore A — groups of four gather around a tub of water. Each group places objects one by one into the water, observes carefully, and records results in textbook page 75 with ticks for float and crosses for sink.

BAND[3] 29-37
  Class comes back together. Teacher asks each group to share one surprising result. Discussion: 'Did anything float that you thought would sink? Why might a feather float?' Teacher guides without giving the scientific explanation fully — wonder is the goal.

BAND[4] 37-40
  Students write one sentence in their notebooks about the most surprising result they found. Teacher collects or reviews briefly as a record of the exploration.

anchor: 'A|beyond_text'   synthesis: None

TEACHER NOTE
  PAPAYA and BANANA are the longest words in the grid and usually the last to be found — if students are stuck after a good search, suggest scanning column by column rather than row by row. The word search works best as a focused individual task followed by a communal check; resist the urge to confirm answers too early. The sentence-writing extension for early finishers (using each fruit word) keeps the activity connected to the writing and word-work the chapter has already practised.

MATERIALS
  ['Textbook p.87']

BAND[0] 0-8
  Teacher reads the eight target fruit words aloud — MANGO, PAPAYA, GRAPES, BANANA, GUAVA, PLUM, PEACH, PEAR — and students repeat each one. Teacher asks which of these fruits appeared in the farmer's story and which were in Chandu's salad, briefly connecting the word list to the chapter's fruit-rich world.

BAND[1] 8-28
  Let us explore — A. (p.87) — students open to the 8×8 word search grid. Teacher explains that words can run horizontally or vertically. Students work individually to find and circle all eight fruit words, calling out 'found one!' quietly when they locate a word. Teacher circulates, pointing quieter students toward the grid's edges where shorter words (PLUM, PEAR) often hide.

BAND[2] 28-36
  Teacher leads a whole-class check: for each of the eight words, one student says the row or column they found it in and teacher confirms. Any word not yet found is located together as a class. Students who finished early write each found word in a sentence of their own on the back of their notebook page.

BAND[3] 36-40
  Closing vocabulary round: teacher points to each fruit word on the board and students call out one fact or association — colour, taste, shape, or where it grows. The unit ends with a brief appreciative reference to the farmer who grew many of these same fruits and whose wise choice is the heart of the chapter's story.

anchor: 'A|reading / A|oracy / A|writing / A|word_work / A|beyond_text'   synthesis: True

TEACHER NOTE
  This synthesis draws together the narrative wisdom of the farmer, the vocabulary of opposites, the grammar of is/are, and the fruit language that runs through the chapter. The writing prompt invites personal application of the story's theme — accept any sincere response, not only fruit-related gifts.

MATERIALS
  ['Textbook pp.78–87', 'Notebooks', 'Pencils']

BAND[0] 0-8
  Teacher invites students to retell the story of the farmer and the king in three to four sentences, drawing on the narrative of the fruit gift, the grape-throwing, and the farmer's famous reply. Two or three students retell aloud while the class listens and adds any missing detail.

BAND[1] 8-18
  Quick oral round: teacher poses three rapid-fire questions drawing on the chapter's ideas — one about the farmer's choice between grapes and watermelons, one using an opposite-word pair from the chapter's vocabulary, and one asking whether a sentence takes 'is' or 'are'. Students respond by raising hands.

BAND[2] 18-30
  Students write three to four sentences in their notebooks on the prompt: 'If you could give a gift to someone special, what would you choose and why?' They use at least one opposite-word pair and the correct form of 'is' or 'are' in their response. Teacher circulates and offers brief verbal feedback.

BAND[3] 30-37
  Students draw and label three fruits from the chapter on a small box in their notebook, writing one sentence about each using 'is' or 'are' correctly — for example, 'Grapes are small and sweet.' This connects word-work learning to the chapter's fruit theme.

BAND[4] 37-40
  Closing reflection: teacher asks 'When something goes wrong, can we find a reason to say Thank God? Why?' One or two students share briefly. Teacher ends by affirming the story's message of wisdom, gratitude, and finding the silver lining.

TEACHER NOTE
  The Do You Know passage positions animal teamwork as a real-world mirror of the poem's message; make this connection explicit when students share — 'Just as the poem says joined hands bring the goal near, the waggle dance brings the whole hive to the flowers.' A common difficulty is students summarising the fact without connecting it to the idea of communication; the paired preparation step is designed to push them past pure recall toward a genuine analogy.

MATERIALS
  ['Textbook pp.7–8']

BAND[0] 0-10
  The teacher reads the Do You Know passage aloud from pp.7–8: honeybees use a waggle dance to share the location of flowers, and ants use signals to find food, defend their colony, and build nests. Students listen with books open.

BAND[1] 10-22
  The teacher leads a whole-class discussion with two oral prompts: 'How is the waggle dance similar to what the poem's team does — cheering and sharing together?' and 'What signal do you think ants might use to say 'danger'?' Students respond freely; the teacher notes interesting ideas on the board.

BAND[2] 22-34
  Students work in pairs: each pair chooses either bees or ants and prepares a short three-sentence spoken explanation — what the creature does together, how they communicate, and one way this resembles teamwork among people. Partners practise saying it to each other before the class share-out.

BAND[3] 34-40
  Four pairs share their explanation with the class — two on bees, two on ants. The teacher closes by asking the class to vote by show of hands: 'Which creature do you think is the better team player — bee or ant?' — keeping the tone light and curious rather than competitive.
