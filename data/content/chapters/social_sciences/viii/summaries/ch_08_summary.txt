Chapter 8: World Geography: Some Glimpses

Introduction

This chapter takes students on a guided tour of the Earth's major physical features, moving from the challenge of representing the planet on paper, to the oceans, and then continent by continent across the world's landforms. It opens by noting that the Earth has long been revered as a mother by indigenous communities and personified in India as Bhūdevī or Vasudhā, and that human lives are deeply connected to its features — the distant Southern Indian Ocean, for instance, influences the monsoon in the Gangetic Plains. The chapter is framed by three Big Questions: what the unique physical features of the continents are, why world geography needs to be understood, and how human actions trigger environmental issues. A student activity introduces the colour language of physical maps, where blue denotes water bodies, green plains, yellow and brown plateaus, dark brown high mountains, light yellow deserts, and white ice and glaciers.

The Complexity of Mapping the Earth

This section explains why every world map is a compromise. Because the Earth is an oblate spheroid, showing it on a flat surface always introduces distortion — a point made through the image of peeling an orange and trying to lay the peel flat without tearing it. The process of representing the Earth on a flat surface is called a map projection, and cartographers have devised several methods. The Mercator Projection preserves shape and direction, making it useful for navigation, but greatly distorts size near the poles, so that Greenland appears as large as Africa; the Robinson Projection is a compromise that balances size and shape distortion for a more realistic-looking world map. The section closes by noting that professionals use the more precise latitude and longitude coordinates introduced in Grade 6, and that understanding the world fairly requires looking at multiple projections rather than one.

The Blue of the Blue Planet, the Oceans

The ocean is introduced as a vast body of saltwater covering about 71 per cent of the Earth's surface and holding 97 per cent of its water — the reason the planet appears blue from space and was called the 'blue planet' by early astronauts. Oceans support life ranging from tiny plankton to the blue whale, and help regulate climate by absorbing heat, releasing it slowly, and adding moisture to the air to form clouds and rainfall. The section notes that the ocean floor is not flat but has mountains, valleys, and plains like the land. A reflective activity asks students to consider what would happen to living beings if pollution harmed plankton, given that oceans supply almost half the oxygen we breathe.

The oceans

Building on Grade 6, this section revisits the five oceans and their distinguishing features: the Pacific as the largest and deepest, home to whales and tuna; the Atlantic as one of the busiest waterways for trade and transport and a rich fishing ground; the Indian Ocean with warm waters, rich biodiversity, and historical importance for international trade; the Arctic as the smallest and shallowest, covered with sea ice and home to polar bears and walruses; and the Southern Ocean encircling Antarctica, with penguins and seals.

Ocean currents

Ocean currents are described as giant rivers flowing through the ocean, divided into warm currents that carry water from the equator toward the poles and cold currents that carry water from the poles toward the equator. These currents affect the climate of coastal areas. The Gulf Stream is used as the worked example: flowing north from near the Equator through the Atlantic Ocean towards Western Europe, its warm water makes places such as the United Kingdom warmer than other locations at the same latitude.

Ocean trenches

This section introduces trenches as very deep, V-shaped areas of the seafloor. The Mariana Trench is the deepest, with near-freezing temperatures, high pressure, and complete darkness making it one of the harshest environments on Earth; it is over 2,000 metres deeper than the height of Mount Everest, and its deepest section, the Challenger Deep, reaches over 11,000 metres below sea level. Scientists studying trenches have found unique life forms that, in the absence of light, derive energy from chemicals in the water rather than photosynthesis — an illustration that life survives even in extreme conditions.

The Great Barrier Reef

A reef is defined as a ridge at or near the ocean surface, formed naturally or artificially, with natural reefs made of rock or built by tiny sea animals called corals. Reefs are among the most diverse ecosystems on Earth and are often called the 'rainforests of the sea' because they provide food and shelter for about a quarter of all ocean species. The Great Barrier Reef, off Australia's northeastern coast, stretches over 2,000 km, covers about 350,000 square kilometres, houses hundreds of coral species and thousands of fish species, and is a UNESCO World Heritage Site. The section stresses the threats reefs face from overfishing, pollution, and climate change, and illustrates coral bleaching as a consequence of changes in ocean chemistry largely caused by human actions.

Smaller water bodies and waterways

This section presents a classification of smaller marine formations through a table: a sea is a large, partly enclosed saltwater area connected to an ocean (Arabian Sea); a gulf is a large inlet cutting deeply into land (Persian Gulf); a bay is a smaller, broad inlet (Bay of Bengal); a strait is a narrow channel joining two seas or oceans (Strait of Malacca); and a canal is a man-made channel connecting water bodies (Suez Canal). Their role in supporting trade and transport is emphasised — the Persian Gulf has carried trade between India and Europe for millennia, bays shelter ports and dockyards from open-ocean winds, the Bay of Bengal is the world's largest bay, and the Suez Canal, opened in 1869, connects the Red Sea with the Mediterranean to create the shortest maritime route from India to Europe.

The Continents: Variety on Land

This section opens the land half of the chapter, recalling from Grade 6 the definitions of mountains, plateaus, and plains, adding deserts as large dry expanses with very little precipitation and distinctive flora and fauna, and defining a continent as a large continuous expanse of land, most commonly counted as seven. Because Part 1 of the textbook covered India's major landforms, the tour deliberately focuses on other parts of the world, spotlighting one or two landforms in each continent and beginning with Asia.

Asia

Roof of the world — the Tibetan Plateau: the highest and largest plateau on Earth, located north of the Himalayan Range and formed by collisions between the Indo-Australian and Eurasian tectonic plates that also raised the Himalayas. It stretches about 2,500 km east to west and 1,000 km north to south at an average height of over 4,500 metres, influences the climate of Asia, and is the source of major rivers including the Indus, Brahmaputra, and Yangtze. Called the 'Third Pole', it holds the largest freshwater reserves outside the polar regions, and climate change has caused snow-melt to expand existing lakes and create new ones — a change students are asked to notice by comparing satellite images from 1987 and 2021. Gobi Desert in the 'Land of the Eternal Blue Sky': deserts are defined as large, extremely dry areas with less than 25 cm of precipitation a year, found on every continent, covering about one-fifth of the Earth's land area and home to around a billion people. The Gobi lies in Mongolia and part of north China; Mongolia is called the 'Land of the Eternal Blue Sky' for its eight to nine sunny months, though winter temperatures can drop to minus 30°C, vegetation is sparse, and herds migrate seasonally to pastures fed by underground water. The Eurasian Steppes: a temperate grassland spread over 8,000 km west to east that acts as a global carbon sink, absorbing and storing carbon underground, and whose water bodies serve as resting spots for migratory birds such as the Siberian crane; the yurt of the steppe nomad and the steppe tortoise illustrate human and animal adaptation. The Urals: this section first explains that mountains are formed by tectonic forces, volcanic activity, and erosion, and that they shape climates, act as 'water towers', form biodiversity hotspots, and often serve as natural boundaries — a student activity asks learners to assess the Himalayas against these roles. The Ural Mountains are an ancient range stretching about 2,500 km from the Arctic Ocean to the Kazakhstan border, forming the natural boundary between Europe and Asia, rich in iron ore, copper, zinc, platinum, gold, coal, oil and natural gas, and supporting coniferous taiga in the west and east, deciduous forest in the south, tundra and swamp in the north, and fertile southern farmland.

Europe

The European Plain: this section explains that plains are vast flatlands formed by sediment deposition with fertile soils supporting farming, trade, and dense populations, while river valleys provide fertile soil, water, and transport routes that nurtured some of the world's earliest civilisations. The European Plain, one of the largest flatlands in the world, spans several countries and is marked by low-lying terrain, gently rolling hills, and wide river valleys. Its fertile soils make it one of the most productive agricultural regions in the world, growing wheat, barley, rye, and potatoes; its rivers have supported trade and transport; and its coal, oil, and natural gas deposits have driven economic growth and the rise of cities such as Moscow, Berlin, Paris, Amsterdam, and Warsaw. The plain has also been the ground for major battles as rulers contested its control. A student activity asks learners to list, from the political map at the end of the textbook, the countries that fall within the European Plain. The section then turns to the crescent-shaped Alps, a natural barrier in south-central Europe containing peaks such as Mont Blanc, with oak and beech forests at lower altitudes, spruce and pine higher up, and alpine flowers such as edelweiss; its wildlife includes ibex, chamois, marmots, golden eagles, lynxes, wolves, and brown bears. The Alps feed the Rhine and tributaries of the Danube, supplying drinking water, irrigation, and hydroelectric power to lowland Europe, and are rich in iron, copper, lead, rock salt, graphite, gypsum, and magnesite.

Africa

The African leg of the tour covers the Sahara Desert, the largest hot desert in the world, where temperatures range from 30°C to 46°C, rainfall is below 25 cm, and vegetation is scarce. It introduces the savannah as a tropical grassland ecosystem of wide open spaces, scattered trees, and seasonal rainfall lying between tropical rainforests and deserts, supporting elephants, lions, zebras, and giraffes, with distinct wet and dry seasons shaping plant growth and animal migration — and now threatened by farming, deforestation, and climate change. Human and biological adaptation in the Sahara is illustrated through oases, underground dwellings that stay cool in summer and warm in winter, nomadic clothing and headgear, date palms, water-storing cacti, and the fennec fox's large heat-dissipating ears. The Sahara has been home to nomadic tribes herding sheep, goats, and camels, and ancient trade routes carried gold, salt, and ideas to the Mediterranean world. The section closes with the Nile, the world's longest river at almost 6,650 km and the cradle of ancient Egyptian civilisation, providing irrigation that turned dry land into fertile fields for wheat, barley, flax, fruits, and vegetables, as well as drinking water, fishing, hydroelectric power, and transport. A student activity asks learners to trace the Nile's course, origin, and direction of flow on the maps provided.

South America

On the west coast lie the Andes Mountains, the continent's largest range, home to Mount Aconcagua, the highest peak in the Western Hemisphere, and to Bolivia's Uyuni Salt Flats, the largest salt flat on Earth. The Andes are a vital freshwater source for millions and feed major rivers including the Amazon, the world's largest river by volume; they hold thousands of species of mammals, birds, amphibians, reptiles, and fish, many unique to the region, but only about a quarter of the original flora and fauna remains, making the range a critical conservation hotspot. The Andes are also resource-rich, holding vast reserves of copper, zinc, silver, and lithium. The Amazon River, longest in South America, flows east from the Andes in Peru to the Atlantic, and its basin supports the Amazon Rainforest, where almost 400 indigenous communities live and help conserve the forest; the forest is threatened by deforestation and climate change, and receives phosphorus carried as dust all the way from the Sahara. Student activities ask why the Amazon is called the 'Lungs of the Earth' and invite group discussion of the short- and long-term effects of deforestation against the region's need for development land. The section ends at the Atacama Desert in Chile, one of the driest places on Earth, stretching about 1,000 km with temperatures varying from 40°C by day to 5°C at night, famous for stargazing and its Mars-like landscape used by NASA to test rovers, but also now one of the world's fastest-growing dumps for discarded clothes driven by fast fashion — a waste problem the UN has called 'an environmental and social emergency'.

North America

The Colorado Plateau is a large highland region in the southwestern United States covering over 3,30,000 square km and containing the Grand Canyon. It is composed mostly of sedimentary rock layers slowly uplifted by tectonic forces and shaped by erosion over millions of years, creating dramatic red rock formations and deep canyons, and is rich in coal, oil, natural gas, uranium, copper, and other minerals; its geology, mineral wealth, and coniferous and deciduous plant life make it important for tourism and resource use, with the Grand Canyon drawing about 5 million visitors annually. The Rocky Mountains stretch over 4,800 km and are rich in copper, gold, and silver, with mining, hydropower, tourism, and agriculture contributing to GDP; they were home to large indigenous Native American communities until about 300 years ago, communities that depended on the region's rivers, forests, and wildlife and regarded the mountains as sacred, and many of whom now live on reservations. Yellowstone National Park in the Rockies is presented as an example of ecosystem restoration, where wolves introduced in 1995 reduced an overgrazing elk population and allowed the flora to return. The Appalachian Mountains are among the oldest ranges in the world, their fertile valleys, abundant game, and natural travel routes enabling indigenous communities such as the Cherokee to thrive; they hold spiritual significance and have supplied coal, timber, and natural gas, with tourism and renewable energy now growing. The Great Lakes are a chain of five massive freshwater lakes vital to North American commerce and industry through their shipping routes and supplying drinking water to several cities, and a vast wildlife habitat facing chemical contamination of sediments and water contamination from agricultural run-off and industrial processes.

The Australian Continent

The final stop notes that a large part of Australia is desert or arid, collectively termed the Great Australian Desert, which includes the Great Victoria Desert. The deserts are home to reptiles, skinks, wallabies, bilbies, and feral camels, with predators including dingoes, large lizards, cats, foxes, and snakes, while rare animals such as the sandhill dunnart, marsupial mole, and mallee fowl struggle to survive. The Spinifex are an indigenous population who have lived in the desert for over 25,000 years, and their way of life and culture, preserved by younger generations, draw tourists alongside the region's geography and wildlife. The section corrects the assumption that all deserts are hot by noting that Antarctica is the largest and coldest desert on Earth, covering over 14 million square kilometres — more than four times the area of India — and is colder than the Arctic, the planet's other polar desert.

Before we move on …

The closing recap consolidates the chapter's argument: the Earth's surface includes varied landforms and water bodies — mountains, plateaus, plains, deserts, grasslands, rivers, and oceans — all shaped by natural processes such as tectonic movements, erosion, and deposition; these features influence climate, ecosystems, and human activities including settlement, agriculture, and trade; human actions such as deforestation, overuse of resources, and pollution disturb this balance and cause desertification, biodiversity loss, and climate change; and studying world geography helps us understand these features and the need to manage them wisely for sustainable development.
