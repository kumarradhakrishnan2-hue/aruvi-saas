Chapter 12: Citizenship: Rights and Duties

Introduction

The chapter opens with Mahatma Gandhi's line that "the true source of rights is duty" and frames the enquiry through three Big Questions: what it means to be a citizen not just legally but in everyday life, why both rights and duties are essential, and how a society can be built where everyone is treated fairly, respected, and included. A prefatory note tells students that citizenship, rights, duties, discrimination, and inclusion are complex ideas with legal meanings that have been deliberately simplified here and will be studied in greater depth in higher grades. The chapter belongs to the Governance and Democracy theme and builds towards the four concepts it names as its organising ideas.

Who Is a Citizen?

This section opens with a school story in which Sonali and Harshada stop bullies teasing a new student, Kruti, about her accent and then invite her onto their badminton team so she feels she belongs — an illustration of what good citizenship looks like in practice. It defines a citizen as someone who legally belongs to a country and is a member of its political community, able to participate in the country's decision-making; in a democracy citizenship confers rights such as voting, free speech, and living and working safely, though these rights have limits, and it carries duties such as obeying the law, paying taxes, respecting others, and helping keep society safe. Citizenship is presented as both a legal identity, formally connecting people to their country, and a social responsibility that creates belonging through shared values, rights, and duties; a "Don't miss out" box distinguishes residence from citizenship, noting that not every resident is a citizen and a citizen may reside abroad, and stresses that citizenship means active participation and the power to question unfair practices. The section notes that in India all citizens are equal under the Constitution despite differences of language, religion, or culture, that the Citizenship Act of 1955 and the Citizenship (Amendment) Act of 2019 set the rules for gaining or losing citizenship, and it introduces the chapter's four concepts — rights (freedoms such as equality, free speech, religion, and education that let people live with dignity), duties (responsibilities such as obeying laws, paying taxes, keeping clean, and helping others), discrimination (unfair treatment based on identity, which citizens must stand against), and inclusion (ensuring no one is left out because of language, religion, culture, ethnicity, caste, or gender), the first three named in the Constitution and inclusion offered as the affirmative way to ensure non-discrimination.

Rights

The section on rights opens with a narrated town-festival scenario in which four denials of rights occur: Meena is told she cannot take part in the debate because "girls should not speak on stage", Ravi, a street vendor's son, is kept out of the library stall while a wealthy boy is welcomed in, some children are made to clean instead of going to school, and Gaganjit, who uses a wheelchair, cannot enter the exhibition hall because there is no ramp. Their teacher explains that the Constitution of India gives every citizen fundamental rights, including the right to equality, the right to freedom of speech and expression, the right to freedom of religion, the right to life and dignity, and the right to education, and that citizens denied these rights may approach the courts for justice. The story resolves as the children speak to the fair organisers and change follows — Meena joins the debate, Ravi enters the library stall, ramps are built for Gaganjit, the working children are enrolled in school with volunteers' help, and all the children appear together on stage at the closing ceremony.

What are rights?

This short section defines rights as what all human beings and members of society are entitled to, while cautioning that not everything a person wants can be called a right. Rights are what people need in order to live with dignity, safety, and respect — the right to education, to earn a living, or to express one's ideas — and they help people grow, learn, and live freely. Actions that cause harm to oneself or others cannot be rights; true rights support the wellbeing and dignity of all, which is why the section concludes that rights are justified claims.

Fundamental Rights in the Indian Constitution

The Constitution guarantees Fundamental Rights — the basic rights every Indian citizen has — set out in six broad categories that protect people's freedom and dignity and that are enforceable by law, so that people can approach the courts if they are violated. A figure names the six: the right to equality (Articles 14–18), the right to freedom (Articles 19–22), the right against exploitation (Articles 23 and 24), the right to freedom of religion (Articles 25–28), cultural and educational rights (Articles 29 and 30), and the right to constitutional remedies (Article 32). The section stresses that fundamental rights protect people not only from unfair treatment by others but also from unfair actions by the government, ensuring fair treatment and preventing the misuse of power.

Right to equality

The right to equality ensures that every person is treated equally before the law, has equal opportunities in jobs and education, and cannot be discriminated against on grounds of religion, race, caste, gender, or place of birth. It also ends practices such as untouchability and prevents the government from conferring special titles on individuals, except for military or academic honours. A "Let's explore" activity contrasts illustrations of equality, where everyone receives exactly the same support, with equity, where everyone gets the support they actually need, and the accompanying text explains that giving the same thing to everyone does not always yield the same benefit because of differences that may be historically rooted in inequality and discrimination; equity is defined as being fair and just in a way that takes account of and seeks to address existing inequalities, with the Beti Bachao Beti Padhao campaign given as an example of a government initiative supporting girls' education, and students are asked to think of others.

Right to freedom

This section presents the right to freedom as one of the most important rights of every Indian, allowing people to express themselves, move freely, and choose how they want to live and work. It emphasises that these freedoms are not unlimited but come with reasonable restrictions so that they are not misused or made harmful to others or to the nation. A "Think about it" activity built around a Sports Day uniform disagreement asks whether a student raising the issue was exercising freedom of speech, whether the concern could have been raised differently, and how schools should handle student opinions.

Right to life

The right to life protects a person's life and liberty and means the right not merely to live but to live with dignity, extending to livelihood, education, health, a clean environment, and fair treatment under the law. The section notes that this right also protects children with disabilities, ensuring they receive the support they need to learn, play, and participate fully on an equal basis with others, with access to education, healthcare, and a safe environment; an accompanying figure shows a tactile map made available in a school for the blind as a way of facilitating the right to education. A "Don't miss out" box extends the idea to nature, describing New Zealand's Whanganui River, which is legally treated as a living being, Costa Rica's constitutional protection of nature's rights, and the Uttarakhand High Court's 2017 declaration of the Ganga and Yamuna as legal entities with appointed guardians — a decision later suspended and overturned by the Supreme Court of India as "legally unsustainable" — and asks students why some people want to give rivers and forests legal rights and how this could help protect the environment.

Right against exploitation

Exploitation is defined as taking unfair advantage of others by denying them basic respect, liberty, and rights, and the section names its common forms: bonded labour, where people are forced to work without fair wages; human trafficking, where people are traded or sold for profit; the abuse or unfair treatment of women and children; and child labour, where children are made to work, often in hazardous situations, instead of going to school. It explains why children need special protection — they depend on adults for safety and wellbeing, may not understand their rights or be able to seek help, and lose education, play, and a healthy childhood when made to work. The right against exploitation makes it illegal to force anyone to work against their will or in unsafe conditions; children below fourteen generally may not be employed, though they may help in non-hazardous family enterprises during vacations or after school hours, and work in factories, mines, and restaurants is banned. A "Don't miss out" box introduces the national child helpline Childline 1098, which began as an action project at an educational institution in the late 1990s, a "Think about it" activity asks students what they would do on seeing a child working instead of being in school, and a real case from 2015 describes twenty children rescued from an embroidery factory after a citizen's call to Childline, with medical care provided and the culprits arrested.

Right to freedom of religion

This section notes that India is a diverse country where people follow many different religions, and that the right to freedom of religion allows every person to choose, practise, and share their religion freely. At the same time it ensures that no one is forced to follow, propagate, or fund a particular religion. State-run and government-aided schools are not allowed to promote any particular religion, which the section explains helps protect India's diversity and keeps all faiths equal in the eyes of the state.

Cultural and educational rights

Cultural and educational rights protect India's cultural diversity and ensure equal access to education for all: communities may preserve their language, script, and traditions, and no student may be denied admission to a government institution because of religion, caste, race, or language. Religious and linguistic minorities also have the right to establish and manage their own schools, so that cultural identity is maintained alongside equality and non-discrimination. The section raises the tension the Constitution holds in balance — protecting minority rights on one hand and upholding equality and non-discrimination on the basis of religion on the other — noting that some people feel minority protections may go against the idea of secularism.

Right to constitutional remedies

The right to constitutional remedies allows citizens to approach the Supreme Court when their fundamental rights are violated, ensuring that these rights are not merely promises on paper but can actually be protected and enforced. A "Let's explore" activity quotes Dr. B.R. Ambedkar from the Constituent Assembly Debates calling this the one article without which the Constitution would be a nullity, "the very soul of the Constitution and the very heart of it", and asks students why it is important to have a way to protect rights.

Key Constitutional Articles That Guide Our Rights

A reference table lists the Fundamental Rights articles, Articles 14 to 32, with a note that students need not memorise them but should be aware of them as citizens of India. It sets out equality before the law (14), no discrimination on grounds of religion, race, caste, sex, or place of birth (15), equal opportunity in public employment (16), abolition of untouchability (17), the end of titles except military and academic distinctions (18), freedom of speech, movement, and peaceful assembly (19), protection of life and personal liberty (21), the right to free and compulsory education for ages six to fourteen (21A), the ban on human trafficking, forced labour, and child labour (23–24), freedom to practise, profess, and manage one's religion (25–28), protection of language, script, and cultural rights along with the rights of minority institutions (29–30), and the right to approach courts to protect Fundamental Rights (32). The table closes by observing that these articles show how the Constitution and the Judiciary together protect the dignity and equality of every citizen.

Duties

The duties half of the chapter opens with a narrative of a Sunday morning community clean-up and improvement drive in Green Park Colony, where children collect plastic wrappers and papers, parents sweep streets and clear clogged drains, residents plant saplings and paint walls with positive messages, older students place bowls of water and food for street dogs, mothers talk to younger children about saving water and electricity, and senior citizens guide everyone. When a local reporter asks the youngest participants why they were spending their Sunday this way, a little girl replies that it is their duty to care for their neighbourhood. The section draws the moral that duties are not only about obeying laws or following rules but about the responsibility each person has towards family, community, country, and the environment, and that a society becomes healthier, happier, and more harmonious when every citizen takes duties seriously.

What are duties?

This section explains that the Constitution not only grants rights but also states what citizens should do as responsible members of society, and that these responsibilities are called fundamental duties, included to remind citizens of their obligations alongside the enjoyment of their fundamental rights. A "Don't miss out" box gives the history: when the Indian Constitution came into effect in 1950 it contained no Fundamental Duties; ten were added in 1976 by a constitutional amendment as Article 51A in Part IV-A; and an eleventh was added in 2002, requiring parents or guardians to ensure that children between the ages of six and fourteen receive an education.

Understanding duties

This section explains that fundamental duties help citizens play an active role in keeping democracy strong and society healthy, reminding them that citizenship is about contributing to the country's progress as well as enjoying rights, and that the duties help maintain good governance, promote unity, protect nature, encourage learning, and build a society where everyone can live with dignity. It then lists all eleven duties of Article 51A, each paired with an everyday school or community example: abiding by and respecting the Constitution, National Flag, and National Anthem; following the ideals of the freedom struggle; protecting India's unity, integrity, and sovereignty; defending the nation and helping in emergencies; promoting harmony among all people and respecting women; preserving culture and heritage; protecting and improving the environment and showing compassion towards animals; developing scientific temper and the spirit of inquiry; protecting public property and avoiding violence; striving for excellence in all activities; and ensuring education for children aged six to fourteen. A "Don't miss out" box asks whether rights and duties are entirely modern concepts, discussing dharma, often translated as duty but with a much wider range of meaning including what is beneficial to people and society; ṛiṇa or debt, the debts owed by birth to devatās, ancestors and parents, gurus, fellow humans, and Nature, whose repayment creates deep connections beyond today's sense of duty; and adhikāra, the word for rights in many Indian languages, which in ancient texts meant capacity, competence, or authority, so that rights depended on one's capacity rather than being universally guaranteed as they are in the Constitution, with dharma implicitly granting everyone the right to justice as the one exception. A "Think about it" activity asks students to name a duty that matches their right to education.

Rights and Duties: Two Sides of the Same Coin

This section draws the two halves of the chapter together, describing rights as the freedoms and benefits every citizen enjoys and duties as the responsibilities every member of a community must follow, and insisting they are not opposites but go together: rights allow people to live with dignity while duties remind them to use freedom wisely and respectfully. It marks the key difference in how the two are secured — rights are protected by law and can be enforced in court even against the government, whereas duties are moral and social responsibilities that citizens are expected to follow though failing to do so may not always lead to legal action — and argues that fulfilling duties helps protect everyone's rights and keeps society fair and peaceful. A paired illustration shows that every right has a matching duty: the right to be treated with respect carries the duty to treat others with respect, and the right to express opinions freely carries the duty to listen to and respect others' views even in disagreement, and to do so without causing harm. A "Let's explore" activity presents everyday situations for students to identify the right or duty involved and asks whether rights can be demanded without doing one's duties.

Discrimination

The discrimination section opens with the story of Sanya, who was excited to audition for the school play but was told by some students that she did not "fit the image" of a lead actor and should try for a smaller role instead. She auditioned anyway, impressed the teachers with her expressive acting and confidence, and was chosen for an important role. The drama teacher afterwards spoke to the class about how excluding or judging someone because of their looks is unfair and a form of discrimination, and the students recognised that talent and effort matter far more than appearance.

Understanding discrimination

Discrimination is defined as mistreating a person or group because of caste, religion, ethnicity, disability, race, physical appearance, gender, sexuality, or economic background — behaviour that is not only unethical but legally prohibited, and that harms the affected person or group both psychologically and physically as well as society as a whole. The section shows how ordinary such conduct can look, from not including a classmate in a group project to not letting someone sit beside you on the school bus because of their social or economic background, and explains that discrimination creates a social barrier that hampers people from enjoying their fundamental rights; it adds that anyone who discriminates ethically ceases to be an active citizen of India, having breached the Constitution's core values of respect and unity. A "Don't miss out" box profiles two women who fought discrimination: Savitribai Phule, who established a school for girls in the mid-nineteenth century at a time when girls' education was at a low ebb and who, being from the Mali community, was abused and had mud thrown at her on her way to school yet remained steadfast, working with her husband Jyotiba Phule so that girls and children from all backgrounds could access education; and Anandibai Joshi, married at nine in 1874 and a mother by fifteen who lost her baby for lack of medical care, and who resolved to study medicine at the Woman's Medical College of Pennsylvania, enduring hardship and disapproval, only to die of illness soon after graduating.

Visible and invisible discrimination

This section teaches that discrimination is not always visible and may be indirect — a jibe, exclusion, or unwillingness to include someone in a project or celebration because of social background, physical appearance, or a perception of intellectual ability. It stresses that the major problem with invisible discrimination is that it is difficult to prove and therefore difficult to penalise. A "Let's explore" activity gives two caselets for small-group discussion — students who mock Rohan's weight and keep him out of their football game, and classmates who ignore Mina after learning that her family cannot afford an overnight educational trip — and asks students to identify the types of discrimination involved, say what they would do, and reflect on discrimination they have faced themselves.

Inclusion

The inclusion section opens with the example of Smt. Amita, who noticed that the number of books borrowed from the school library had dropped and started a reading club to revive the habit of reading. When only a few confident readers of English joined, she expanded the club's scope to include books in the regional language. Membership soon increased and the club's activities grew to include storytelling and puppetry from the region, so that all children began to participate irrespective of their fluency in a particular language.

Understanding inclusion

Inclusion is defined as creating an environment where everyone feels welcomed, respected, valued, and included, with equal access to opportunities — especially important for groups historically excluded because of their social or economic background. The section stresses that inclusion goes beyond giving reservations: it means making sure everyone feels accepted and supported, and in the Constitution fundamental rights and duties work together to support it. Practical examples include finding ways for people speaking different languages to communicate, welcoming students of all castes, religions, and backgrounds into schools, providing extra help such as braille books, ramps, or classroom support for children with special needs, and removing barriers to participation; an accompanying illustration asks students to compare inclusion with the earlier equality and equity figure and note the difference. A "Think about it" activity asks what students would do about a classmate who takes longer to read or write because of a different way of learning and is left out of group work, and what steps they could take to make their classroom more inclusive.

Before we move on ...

The closing recap consolidates the chapter's argument: being a responsible, empathetic, and active citizen means living your rights while respecting others' rights; the Constitution guarantees fundamental rights and one can go to court for remedies if they are violated; discrimination based on caste, religion, gender, or personal preferences such as choice of clothing is against fundamental rights; rights and duties work together and one cannot be fulfilled while the other is ignored; deliberate actions are needed to prevent discrimination and ensure inclusion; actions matter, so citizens should speak up against unfairness, follow the law, and help others feel respected and included; democracy is strengthened by enabling and facilitating the participation of all; a peaceful society is built through everyday choices, words, and actions; and listening with empathy and acting with responsibility helps everyone feel valued and safe.
