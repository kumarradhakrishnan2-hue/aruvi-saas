Chapter 9: India's Long Road to Independence

Introduction

This chapter offers a brief account of India's nearly century-long struggle against British rule, opening with a quotation from Sri Aurobindo (1909) declaring Swaraj or absolute autonomy as the ideal, and with an image of a women's procession in Bombay during the 1942 Quit India Movement. It is framed by three Big Questions: how a land of diverse geographies, languages and religions came together to end nearly two centuries of British rule; what the main developments and important figures of the freedom struggle were; and what issues the leading freedom fighters agreed or disagreed upon. An illustrated timeline runs from 1850 to 1950, marking events such as the Crown's takeover in 1858, the founding of the Indian National Congress in 1885, the 1905 Partition of Bengal, the 1907 Surat split, Gandhi's return in 1915, the Non-Cooperation, Civil Disobedience and Quit India movements, the Indian National Army, and Independence and Partition in 1947. The chapter warns that the full story would take many books — the Indian Council of Historical Research's five-volume Dictionary of Martyrs lists some 14,000 heroes and heroines — so the few named here stand for many more, and it introduces the term martyr and the idea that freedom fighters shared one objective but often differed on the means. A student activity asks learners to talk to grandparents or elderly family members about what they remember of the freedom struggle and to discuss in class how people followed different paths to the same goal.

A Royal Proclamation

Following the Great Uprising of 1857 studied in Part 1, the British Crown under Queen Victoria took direct control of India from the East India Company in 1858, with British India governed by the Secretary of State for India in London and the Viceroy, also called Governor General, in India. A royal proclamation issued in Victoria's name promised to respect the rights, dignity and honour of some 560 native princes, who would continue to rule under British supremacy or 'paramountcy', and assured Indians of equal treatment and no imposition of Christianity — assurances that were not respected in practice, as Residents or Political Agents interfered in princely administration and missionary activity continued with official encouragement. Economic exploitation continued: heavy land taxes produced severe famines and discontent among peasants and tribals, and local industries could not compete with cheap British manufactures. Racial discrimination was systematic — lower pay for Indians in the army, civil services and universities, British-majority juries lenient towards European crimes, exclusion from clubs, trains and public spaces, the 1878 Arms Act barring Indians from keeping arms, and the 1878 Vernacular Press Act censoring the Indian-language press. A student activity asks learners to return to the map of the British Indian Empire in Part 1 and recall which territories were directly administered and which were princely states.

Reform Movements

This short section explains that while British rule set off intense debates within Indian society and sections of the elite looked to Europe as a model for 'modernising' India, Indian reformers also drew on European models while challenging the lack of formal education and social practices such as child marriage, the degraded status of widows, and caste-based discrimination. A table presents a glimpse of a few reformers and their movements: the Brahmo Samaj (earlier Brahmo Sabha), founded by Raja Ram Mohan Roy in Calcutta in 1828, which opposed child marriage and caste discrimination and supported widow rights, girls' education, a monotheistic reading of Hinduism and English education; the Arya Samaj, founded by Swami Dayananda Saraswati in Bombay in 1875, which emphasised a return to the Vedas, rational thinking, equality and self-reliance and inspired many later freedom fighters in north India; the late nineteenth-century Shuddhi Movement of Arya Samaj leaders; and other reformers such as Iswar Chandra Vidyasagar, Syed Ahmed Khan, and Jyotirao and Savitribai Phule, who promoted modern education for girls and marginalised groups. The section stresses that there were many more reformers across India.

Other Influences

Before turning to the political front, the chapter examines two cultural currents that fed nationalist feeling. Bankim Chandra Chattopadhyay, a sub-magistrate in Bengal in the 1860s, wrote popular Bengali novels on romance and Indian history and in 1882 published Anandamath, set amid the Sannyasi Rebellion and the 1770 Bengal famine, in which sannyasis worship the motherland as the divine mother and recite the poem Bande Mataram — composed earlier in 1875, set to music and sung by Rabindranath Tagore at the 1896 Calcutta Congress session, and adopted as India's National Song by the Constituent Assembly in 1950. The second figure is Narendranath Datta, born in 1863, who became Swami Vivekananda as a disciple of Sri Ramakrishna, made a powerful defence of India's civilisation at the 1893 Parliament of the World's Religions in Chicago, founded the Ramakrishna Mission combining spiritual practice with social service, and though not politically active urged Indians to overcome fear and self-doubt with the call to "Arise, awake, and stop not till the goal is reached"; he encouraged science, advising Jamsetji Tata to create what became the Indian Institute of Science, while rejecting blind imitation of the West, and his birthday, 12 January, is observed as National Youth Day. Student activities invite learners to work out the meaning of the first two stanzas of Vande Mataram and to discuss the relevance of Vivekananda's values in the twenty-first century.

The Rise of the Indian National Congress

This section traces the beginnings of organised political activity. Allan Octavian Hume, a British officer who had become critical of colonial policies as the cause of Indian poverty, resigned in 1882 and brought together like-minded Indians, leading to the first session of the Indian National Congress in Bombay in 1885, with Dadabhai Naoroji playing a leading role and Womesh Chunder Bonnerjee elected first president; seventy-two delegates from diverse regions and religions attended, mostly from Anglicised upper classes. The Congress began as a forum professing full faith in the 'providential character' of British rule and 'unswerving allegiance to the British Crown', holding yearly sessions in different cities and passing resolutions seeking dialogue, greater Indian participation in governance, protection of civil rights, and social and economic reforms. The colonial government largely ignored these resolutions, granting only minor concessions and denying Indians any meaningful share in governance.

Growing Discontent

This section gives a few examples of the discontent that kept growing after 1857. In Punjab in the 1860s and early 1870s, Baba Ram Singh amplified the Kuka or Namdhari Movement, which worked against female infanticide, child marriage and the dowry system, rejected foreign goods, wore hand-spun white clothes, and set up an independent postal system and private courts; after Kukas attacked government-sanctioned slaughterhouses in 1872, sixty-five were executed without trial, many blown from cannons, and Baba Ram Singh died in exile in Burma, leaving a legacy that anticipated later boycott movements. In western Maharashtra, famine and the 1896 bubonic plague and the authorities' harsh control measures led the three Chapekar brothers to shoot dead the British Plague Commissioner of Poona and a British officer in 1897; they were betrayed, arrested and hanged. Bal Gangadhar Tilak, a founding member of the Congress who found its leaders too moderate, attacked colonial oppression in his Marathi newspaper Kesari, turned Ganesh Chaturthi and the Shivaji festival into popular events using religious and patriotic symbols, was jailed for eighteen months on a sedition charge, came to be called 'Lokmanya', and translated and interpreted the Bhagavad Gita in the work published in English as The Gita Rahasya. The section also notes the secret societies and revolutionary groups of the period — the Anushilan Samiti founded in Bengal in 1902 with Aurobindo Ghose and his brother Barindra Kumar Ghose trying to coordinate its work, and V.D. Savarkar's Abhinav Bharat — and closes with Birsa Munda of the Chota Nagpur region, who led the 1899–1900 uprising known as 'Ulgulaan' or the Great Tumult against colonial authority, oppressive landlords and missionary inroads, and died in jail in 1900 at the age of 24.

The 1905 Partition of Bengal and National Awakening

Viceroy George Curzon's plan to partition the Bengal presidency was officially justified as administrative efficiency but was in reality an application of 'divide and rule', splitting the largely Muslim east from the largely Hindu west; despite widespread opposition the partition was announced in 1905. It provoked outrage, with Rabindranath Tagore, Surendranath Banerjee, Bipin Chandra Pal and Ashwini Kumar Dutt uniting people across communities, and had two visible results: a sharpened communal division, as most Muslim leaders defended the partition, and an unplanned national awakening of meetings, processions and protest rallies in Calcutta, the singing of Vande Mataram in the streets until the authorities banned it, and the tying of rakhis as a symbol of Hindu–Muslim unity. Two programmes of mass action emerged — Swadeshi ('of one's own country') and boycott — encouraging the rejection of foreign goods, bonfires of foreign cloth and sugar, and the wearing of Indian-spun fabric as a symbol of self-reliance, while the Boycott movement extended to British institutions such as government schools, colleges and courts, with limited impact because Indian institutions were not ready to replace them. The section notes that Swadeshi was not new, citing Jamsetji Tata's Swadeshi Mills in Bombay (1886), the Punjab National Bank founded with Indian capital in Lahore in 1895 with Lala Lajpat Rai among its founding directors, and Prafulla Chandra Ray's Bengal Chemicals and Pharmaceuticals Ltd in 1901.

The Nationalist Agenda

In 1906 Bipin Chandra Pal launched the English daily Bande Mataram, which Aurobindo Ghose soon took charge of, spreading the idea of India's freedom; an extract from a September 1906 article argues that the agitation was not merely against Partition but for 'absolute national autonomy'. The paper added National Education to Swadeshi and boycott to counter the denationalising effects of English education, and spelt out a 'doctrine of passive resistance'; the same year a National Council of Education was created and the Bengal National College launched with Aurobindo Ghose as its first principal, an institution later integrated into Jadavpur University. The programme received a boost in December 1906 when the Calcutta Congress session, with Dadabhai Naoroji as president, declared Swaraj, Swadeshi, Boycott and National Education to be the Congress's objectives — though 'Swaraj' was left undefined, ranging in meaning from self-rule within the British Empire to full independence. Student activities ask why the daily avoided the word 'independence', invite comment on the phrase 'absolute national autonomy', and ask learners to observe the boxes on either side of the daily's title in the reproduced front page.

India as the 'Mother'

This section examines the recurring theme of 'Mother India' or 'Bharat Mata' in Nationalist speeches and writings, partly inspired by Bankim's Anandamath, in which India was seen as a divine motherland. In 1905, at the time of Bengal's partition, the painter Abanindranath Tagore produced his painting of 'Bharat Mata'; in the Madras presidency the poet and freedom fighter Subramania Bharati translated Bande Mataram into Tamil and composed many poems calling on Mother India to rise; and in Punjab, Lala Lajpat Rai started a publication titled Vande Mataram. Student activities ask learners to compare the two representations of Mother India and to describe the details on the cover of Bharati's magazine Vijaya.

The All-India Muslim League

In December 1906, prominent leaders of the Muslim community founded the All-India Muslim League in Dhaka to defend Muslim political interests in British India, their concerns having grown since the formation of the Congress, which they distrusted for fear that Muslims would not be adequately represented in a largely Hindu-dominated body. The section recalls that Syed Ahmad Khan had campaigned against the Congress and founded the All India Muhammadan Educational Conference in 1886 as an alternative political platform, and that Congress attempts to address these concerns had little success. It notes that the League initially represented a limited, mainly elite segment of the Muslim population, that quite a few Muslims had opposed the partition of Bengal, and that a few decades later this division would contribute to the emergence of the Two-Nation Idea.

The 1907 Surat Split

Alarmed by the Calcutta session's call for Swaraj, the British authorities pressured moderate Congress leaders, and at the Surat session of December 1907 these Moderates — among them Dadabhai Naoroji, Gopal Krishna Gokhale, founder of the Servants of India Society, Pherozeshah Mehta, Surendranath Banerjee and Rashbehari Ghosh — tried to step back from the Calcutta resolutions and return to requests for minor concessions and gradual self-government within the Empire. When Tilak was prevented from speaking, chaos descended on the session and the Nationalist camp led by Tilak, Lala Lajpat Rai, known as the 'Lion of Punjab', and Aurobindo Ghose walked out to hold a separate session. The section explains the labels of the period: the Moderates and the British called those demanding nothing less than independence 'Extremists' or 'Radicals', while the group called themselves simply 'Nationalists', seeing nothing extreme in the objective of an independent Indian nation.

The Revolutionaries

This section covers the turn to armed action from the late nineteenth century, beginning with the growth of a critical press — Kesari and Bande Mataram, the more moderate Voice of India of Dadabhai Naoroji and The Bengalee of Surendranath Banerjee, and the radical Bengali paper Yugantar ('New Era'), run by revolutionaries led by Barindra and linked to the Anushilan Samiti, which preached violent overthrow of British rule. In April 1908, Khudiram Bose and Prafulla Chaki attempted to assassinate the British judge Douglas Kingsford but killed two British women instead; Prafulla shot himself, and Khudiram, aged 18, was hanged and became a national hero. The Alipore Bomb trial followed, in which Aurobindo Ghose, defended by Chittaranjan Das, was acquitted while Barindra and another were sentenced to death, commuted to life in the Cellular Jail; the authorities then suppressed the Nationalist press and jailed Tilak for six years in Mandalay in a sedition case in which Muhammad Ali Jinnah was his lawyer. The section records further attacks — the 1909 bomb thrown at Viceroy Minto's carriage in Ahmedabad, the Nashik assassination of a district magistrate by Anant Kanhere, Krishnaji Karve and Vinayak Deshpande of Abhinav Bharat, Vanchinathan's shooting of a district collector near Tirunelveli in 1911 in connection with the persecution of V.O. Chidambaram Pillai's Swadeshi Steam Navigation Company, and the 1912 bomb thrown at Viceroy Charles Hardinge during his ceremonial entry into Delhi — together with the annulment of the partition of Bengal in 1911. It then describes the Ghadar Movement of 1914–15, an attempt by the Ghadar Party, founded by Indian migrants in North America including Lala Hardayal and Sohan Singh Bhakna, to trigger a mutiny in the British Indian Army during World War I, with Rash Behari Bose, Sachindra Nath Sanyal and Kartar Singh Sarabha working to infiltrate the army until British intelligence uncovered the plot, and the 'German Plot' of Jatin Mukherjee, known as Bagha Jatin, who died after a 75-minute gunbattle at Balasore in September 1915. Boxed notes describe the Cellular Jail in the Andaman Islands, known as 'Kala Pani', its solitary confinement, forced labour and brutality as recorded by Barindra Ghose and V.D. Savarkar; the freedom struggle in the North-East, including the Khasi Revolt led by Tirot Sing, the Singpho Rebellion, the hanging of Maniram Dewan and Piyali Baruah, the Quit India martyrs Kanak Lata Baruah and Kushal Konwar, and Rani Gaidinliu of Manipur; and Aurobindo Ghose's 1910 withdrawal to Chandernagore and then Pondicherry, where he became known as Sri Aurobindo. Student activities ask learners to explore freedom fighters of the North-East and to interpret two of Tilak's statements, including "Swaraj is my birthright and I shall have it."

Home Rule Movement and the Lucknow Pact

Released from jail in 1914 with his health weakened, Tilak set himself three tasks: to reunite the two Congress factions, to promote Hindu–Muslim cooperation, and to work towards home rule. The first was achieved at the 1916 Lucknow session, where the Lucknow Pact was also concluded between the Congress and the All-India Muslim League, with the Congress accepting separate electorates for Muslims in elections to the provincial and central legislative councils. The same year Tilak, together with Annie Besant, an Irish philosopher, reformer and activist committed to Indian self-government, launched the Home Rule Movement demanding self-government within the British Empire as a practical first step; the British refused even this, and the struggle was poised to enter a new phase of mass political mobilisation.

Gandhi and the Non-Cooperation Movement

Mohandas Karamchand Gandhi, born in Porbandar, Gujarat, in 1869, studied law in London and then went to South Africa, where his experience of racial discrimination and of the hardships of the Indian community led him to develop satyagraha ('insistence on truth'), based on passive resistance, nonviolence or ahimsa, and the acceptance of suffering in the quest for truth. Addressed by then as 'Mahatma', he returned to India in 1915 and joined the Congress on Gopal Krishna Gokhale's advice, applying satyagraha three years later to local grievances — the exploited indigo farmers of Bihar, low-paid mill workers in Ahmedabad, and peasants in Kheda burdened by excessive land revenue. The section also notes Vallabhbhai Patel's leadership of the Bardoli movement of 1928 for peasant rights, one of the most successful grassroots movements of the freedom struggle, for which Gandhi gave him the title 'Sardar'.

The Non-Cooperation Movement

The end of World War I brought no freedom: wartime food exports caused shortages, defence expenditure drove up prices, and the Rowlatt Act of 1919 allowed detention without trial and effectively curtailed civil liberties. Gandhi launched a nationwide protest with a day of prayer and fasting, urged Hindu–Muslim unity in satyagraha, promoted Swadeshi goods as a 'religious duty', and encouraged nonviolent boycott of foreign goods, schools and courts, promising Swaraj within a year if nonviolence was strictly maintained. After Tilak's death in August 1920, Gandhi became the undisputed mass leader of the Congress, which formally launched the Non-Cooperation Movement at its Nagpur session in December 1920 with support from leaders including Vallabhbhai Patel, and the movement soon drew mass participation across India. A reflective box discusses the photograph of Gandhi spinning khadi as a symbol of Swadeshi and self-reliance, a source of work for impoverished villagers and a direct challenge to British textile imports.

The Jallianwala Bagh Massacre and its Aftermath

On 13 April 1919, thousands of men, women and children gathered in the garden of Jallianwala Bagh in Amritsar, some to celebrate Baisakhi and others to protest peacefully against the arrest of local leaders and the Rowlatt Act, when Brigadier-General Dyer and his troops sealed the main exit and fired about 1,650 rounds for some ten minutes, deliberately aiming at the thickest parts of the crowd; many were shot, some trampled, and others leapt into a well. Official British counts admitted nearly 400 dead and over 1,200 wounded while Indian estimates exceeded 1,000, and the massacre permanently scarred public trust in the British government and united Indians of all backgrounds in the resolve to attain freedom. Dyer, known as the 'Butcher of Amritsar', defended his action before a British-dominated inquiry committee that condemned it without recommending penal action, and was eventually removed from India; the British government has still not apologised, describing it only as a 'deeply shameful event in British history'. The section closes with Punjab's lieutenant governor O'Dwyer, who defended Dyer and was shot dead in London two decades later by Udham Singh of the Ghadar Party, who asserted the legitimacy of his action at his trial and was hanged. A student activity presents Rabindranath Tagore's letter of protest to the Viceroy, in which he returned his knighthood, and asks what he meant by saying the rulers 'congratulated themselves'.

The Khilafat Movement and its Aftermath

The section explains that the Turkish-led Ottoman Empire had for some six centuries spanned much of southeast Europe, western Asia and north Africa, its sultan in Constantinople also serving as Caliph or politico-religious head of the Muslim world, and that the empire's dismemberment after World War I left the Caliph's position shaky. In India, Muhammad Ali and Shaukat Ali, the Ali brothers, and Maulana Abul Kalam Azad launched the Khilafat Movement in 1919 to preserve the Caliph's religious authority; Gandhi saw an opportunity for Hindu–Muslim unity, and in 1920 an alliance was forged between Khilafat leaders and the Congress, merging the movement with Non-Cooperation and initially increasing Muslim participation in the national movement. Criticism followed from Congress members who felt the Caliph's status was of no concern to India and from Muslim leaders such as Syed Ahmad Khan who objected to tying Indian Muslim loyalty to a religious power in Turkey, while Jinnah left the Congress at its 1920 session after warning against mixing religion with the independence movement. In February 1922, after protesters at Chauri Chaura in present-day Uttar Pradesh killed policemen by setting a station on fire, Gandhi called off the Non-Cooperation Movement as 'inhuman conduct', a decision disapproved of by Motilal Nehru, Jawaharlal Nehru, Chittaranjan Das and Subhas Chandra Bose, and Motilal Nehru and Das formed the short-lived Swaraj Party; the Khilafat Movement itself died when Mustafa Kemal abolished the Turkish sultanate in 1922 and the Caliphate in 1924, leaving a debated legacy of temporary unity alongside a reinforced sense of separateness and subsequent Hindu-Muslim riots.

Protests, Repression and Revenge

Aware of growing popular discontent and of what crores of revolting Indians could do, the British repeatedly offered reforms that stopped short of home rule, such as the Simon Commission which came to India in 1927 to study constitutional reform without a single Indian member, provoking rallies, black flags and the slogan 'Simon Go Back!'. In Lahore in October 1928, Lala Lajpat Rai led a nonviolent march that the police brutally lathi-charged; badly injured, he addressed a meeting the same evening declaring that the blows struck at him would be the last nails in the coffin of British rule, and he died two weeks later, further inflaming national sentiment. The Simon Commission thus laid bare once again the British refusal to consult Indians about their own future and strengthened calls for complete self-rule.

More revolutionary actions

Disagreeing with Gandhi's nonviolence and its slow pace, many freedom fighters held that force was a legitimate means to independence. The Hindustan Republican Association, inspired by the Ghadar Movement, funded its activities through the Kakori Train Robbery near Lucknow led by Ram Prasad Bismil, Ashfaqulla Khan and Chandrashekhar Azad, which provoked a massive crackdown and the execution of Bismil, Khan and several others, who became popular heroes at their trial. Reorganised as the Hindustan Socialist Republican Association, its leaders Chandrashekar Azad and Bhagat Singh sought to avenge Lala Lajpat Rai's death and in December 1928 assassinated a younger British officer by mistake; a year later Bhagat Singh and an associate dropped two low-intensity bombs in Delhi's Central Legislative Assembly, intending only 'to make the deaf hear', and after a long hunger strike against the treatment of Indian prisoners he was hanged in 1931 with Sukhdev Thapar and Shivaram Rajguru, becoming, in Subhas Chandra Bose's words, a symbol of an unconquerable spirit of revolt. The section notes that there were many more revolutionaries including women and even schoolgirls: Suniti Chowdhury and Shanti Ghosh assassinated a District Magistrate in Comilla in 1931, and Bina Das, a Calcutta University student, narrowly missed the Governor of Bengal at a convocation and was sentenced to nine years' rigorous imprisonment.

Purna Swaraj and Civil Disobedience

In 1928 a report drafted under Motilal Nehru's chairmanship, reacting to the Simon Commission, called for Dominion status — total internal autonomy with Britain retaining military and foreign policy and the British monarch as head of state, as in Canada and Australia — but with Britain unwilling to grant even this, younger leaders such as Jawaharlal Nehru and Subhas Chandra Bose pressed for Purna Swaraj or complete independence, a demand earlier voiced by the Nationalist faction, by Aurobindo in 1908, by Maulana Hasrat Mohani in 1921 and by V.D. Savarkar in 1925. The resolution demanding Purna Swaraj was passed at the Lahore session in December 1929, and an extract is quoted asserting the inalienable right of the Indian people to freedom and the right to alter or abolish an oppressive government. To implement it, Gandhi launched the Civil Disobedience Movement with the Salt March of March–April 1930, a 380-km walk to Dandi in southern Gujarat where he symbolically collected salt, chosen because the colonial government held a monopoly on salt production and taxed it heavily. Thousands including Gandhi were arrested, but the movement spread with salt marches elsewhere, the boycott of foreign cloth and refusal to pay taxes, and large numbers of women broke the salt law and picketed liquor and foreign-cloth shops — among them Sarojini Naidu, poet, orator and the first Indian woman president of the Congress in 1925 — while the Qissa Khwani Bazaar massacre at Peshawar on 23 April 1930 saw British troops fire on a crowd protesting the arrest of Abdul Ghaffar Khan, with two platoons of the Royal Garhwal Rifles under Chandra Singh Garhwali refusing to fire and choosing jail instead. A student activity asks learners to form groups to analyse and compare the demands contained in the Purna Swaraj resolution.

The Gandhi-Irwin Pact

In 1931, seeking to appease the protests, Viceroy Irwin agreed with Gandhi to release political prisoners not involved in violence and to allow limited private manufacture of salt, in return for which the Congress suspended civil disobedience and agreed to attend a Round Table Conference in London; the pact marked the first formal negotiation between the British government and the Congress as equals, but the conference produced no breakthrough. Criticised by younger Congress leaders for not guaranteeing Purna Swaraj, the pact was followed by Gandhi's attempt to resume civil disobedience, met with massive arrests and waning public enthusiasm, and he withdrew the movement in 1934, after which the Congress turned to constitutional methods; in 1935 the British conceded provincial autonomy with elected provincial governments, while retaining foreign affairs, defence and internal security and allowing governors to take over provincial administration indefinitely. During this period Gandhi focused on social reform, especially his campaign against untouchability, and clashed with B.R. Ambedkar, who had himself suffered such discrimination and believed separate electorates were the only way to secure a political voice for the Depressed Classes, now called Scheduled Castes; Gandhi feared this would permanently divide Hindu society and began a 'fast unto death', and the resulting Poona Pact replaced separate electorates with an increased number of reserved seats in the legislatures.

The Idea of Separate Nations

Muslims generally kept away from the Civil Disobedience Movement, and when the Urdu philosopher and poet Muhammad Iqbal presided over the 1930 All-India Muslim League session at Allahabad he proposed a separate territory for Muslims in northwestern India within a larger Indian federation, which would allow them to preserve their culture and religious identity. Though this was not yet the precise idea of Pakistan — the word was coined a few years later — it intensified the debate about separateness. Nearly all Congress leaders, including Muslim leaders such as Maulana Azad and Abdul Ghaffar Khan, opposed the Two-Nation idea and insisted that Hindus and Muslims could live together, while Jinnah endorsed the idea of Pakistan; a few Hindu organisations such as the Hindu Mahasabha under V.D. Savarkar also saw Hindus and Muslims as two distinct nations living side by side, though without explicitly asking for partition.

World War II and the Indian National Army

India was pulled into the war that began in 1939 without being consulted, and it placed an enormous strain on the country: more than 2.5 million Indian soldiers joined, forming the largest volunteer army in history, with at least 87,000 losing their lives, while at home shortages, high taxes and the export of grain on Winston Churchill's orders contributed to the horrific Bengal famine of 1943 in which millions perished. Support for the British was initially widespread, but many Indians came to feel they were making huge sacrifices for a war that was not their own while their own freedom was denied. Subhas Chandra Bose, once a Congress leader, broke away from Gandhi's nonviolence, convinced that freedom would come only through armed struggle; escaping house arrest in early 1941, he travelled through Afghanistan and Russia to Germany, then to Indonesia, Japan and finally Singapore in 1943, where he formed the Indian National Army from Indian prisoners of war and volunteers and declared the Azad Hind ('free India') government. The INA fought alongside the Japanese army in Burma and northeastern India, particularly Manipur and Nagaland, but was militarily defeated and failed to establish a base in India; many in India admired Bose's defiance while others criticised his alliance with Nazi Germany and Japan.

The Quit India Movement

Under pressure to secure India's support during the war, Churchill sent Stafford Cripps to India in 1942 with an offer of dominion status after the war, but with a provision allowing provinces to opt out of post-war India; a few leaders such as C. Rajagopalachari favoured tactical acceptance, but most, including Gandhi, found the assurances insufficient and rejected it. In August 1942 Gandhi launched the Quit India Movement with the call "Do or Die" and the demand that the British leave India at once; although virtually the entire Congress leadership was arrested, mass protests and rallies erupted nationwide, workers struck, students left schools, government buildings were attacked and telegraph poles uprooted, with Ram Manohar Lohia and Jayaprakash Narayan instrumental in keeping the underground movement going, and Viceroy Linlithgow called it "by far the most serious rebellion since 1857". Brutally suppressed with many deaths and numerous arrests, it nonetheless signalled that the British could no longer rule India without Indian consent. Boxed notes describe Aruna Asaf Ali, who hoisted the national flag at Gowalia Tank Maidan in Bombay when senior leaders were arrested, went underground for nearly four years editing underground bulletins and later supported the Royal Indian Navy mutiny of February 1946, and Kanaklata Barua, the 17-year-old Assamese activist shot dead while leading a flag-hoisting procession at Gohpur, honoured as 'Birangana'. A reflective box gives the numbers jailed during the Non-Cooperation, Salt Satyagraha, Civil Disobedience and Quit India movements and invites a class discussion on the significance of this collective sacrifice.

Partition, Turmoil, and Independence

World War II ended in 1945 with the Allies' victory over the Axis, and as many European powers realised they could no longer hold their colonies an era of decolonisation began, with a war-exhausted Britain finally seeking a transition to India's independence. A mutiny in the Royal Indian Air Force in January 1946 involving many thousands of personnel, followed the next month by the Royal Indian Navy mutiny in Bombay, where naval ratings rebelled against ill-treatment and the revolt spread across India, signalled that the British could no longer rely on the Indian military to hold the empire. The Cabinet Mission of 1946 then sought to plan a peaceful transfer of power by dividing India into three groups of provinces, each with complete autonomy except in defence, foreign affairs and communication, but complex talks ended in deadlock because of deep divisions between the Congress and the Muslim League.

'Direct Action'

As the demand for a separate Muslim state grew intense, Jinnah openly declared a break from constitutional methods and called for 'Direct Action Day' on 16 August 1946, urging Muslims to demonstrate for the cause of Pakistan and stating, "We will either have a divided India or a destroyed India." A wave of brutal communal violence, encouraged by provocative speeches and pamphlets, submerged Calcutta, with thousands killed as Muslim mobs attacked Hindus who then retaliated and thousands more displaced; the violence created a deep sense of fear that made peaceful coexistence seem impossible and partition unavoidable. An Interim Government led by Jawaharlal Nehru took charge in September 1946 to manage the transition, but tensions remained high while hurried negotiations pushed the British towards a quick exit.

The Partition of India

This section argues that the British had for decades encouraged Hindu–Muslim differences to grow, that partition resulted from disagreements between the two communities or rather small sections of them, that the plan was widely opposed even by the Congress, that there was no national consensus, and that whether it was the only way forward has been debated ever since. In July 1947 Mountbatten, the last Viceroy, chose Cyril Radcliffe, a British lawyer unfamiliar with India, and gave him five weeks to draw the Radcliffe Line, splitting Punjab and Bengal almost overnight and creating a Hindu-majority India and a Muslim-majority Pakistan comprising West Pakistan and East Pakistan, the latter independent as Bangladesh since 1971; millions found themselves on the 'wrong' side of the new borders. The Partition of August 1947 unleashed unprecedented chaos, migration and violence, fuelled by mistrust, a rushed boundary line and a breakdown of law and order, with organised gangs, rumours and a desire to 'cleanse' new territories driving massacres in which women were especially targeted, and Gandhi lamented that the violence secretly harboured had come back on the nation. The section also recounts how the more than 560 princely states were given the option to join India or Pakistan or technically to remain independent — a classic colonial step against India's integrity — and how Sardar Vallabhbhai Patel used persuasion, firmness and, as a last resort, military action to integrate them, earning the title 'Iron Man of India'. With independence on 15 August 1947 India awoke to freedom at midnight under Prime Minister Jawaharlal Nehru and Governor-General Mountbatten, though joy was shadowed by the sorrow of Partition, and the section closes by noting that historians have moved from crediting Britain's exit mainly to Gandhi and the Congress to recognising multiple factors — popular uprisings, revolutionary attempts, the air force and navy mutinies, Britain's diminished post-war status, and the worldwide trend of decolonisation. A reflective box lists many more freedom fighters who could not be named in the chapter and asks students to add names and to acknowledge the sacrifices of the women in freedom fighters' families, before the closing observation that from 1857 to 1947, for ninety years, generations of Indians fought and sacrificed, and that India's freedom was a hard-won collective victory raising the question of what sort of nation free India would build.

Before we move on …

The closing recap consolidates the chapter's argument: India's struggle for independence from colonial rule was long and complex, with ups and downs and immense suffering and sacrifice; there were multiple approaches to the same objective — Moderates who sought a degree of autonomy within the British Empire through gradual reform, Nationalists who worked for independence and mass awakening through Swaraj, Swadeshi and boycott, revolutionaries who believed violent acts would unsettle the colonial power and lead to an armed uprising, and Gandhi's nonviolence, satyagraha and mass mobilisation, among others; the movement unified the subcontinent but could not resolve the political Hindu-Muslim question, as some Muslim leaders insisted on separateness; and the journey left a complex legacy of both triumph and tragedy, since 1947 brought the joy of independence alongside the deep scar of Partition's violence and displacement.
