Chapter 10: A Journey Through Indian Architecture

Introduction

This chapter takes students on a guided journey through Indian architecture, defined as the art and techniques of design and construction combining functional and aesthetic aspects — a field that is both artistic and highly technical, and in which India has excelled for several millennia. It opens with a quotation from Ananda Coomaraswamy on the craftsman as part of the universe rather than an individual expressing personal whims, and explains that the evolution of forms, their diversity and technicalities cannot be covered exhaustively, so the chapter instead visits a few representative structures and monuments grouped into a few categories. Three Big Questions frame the chapter: what role architecture has played in Indian civilisation, what the key concepts and developments behind Indian architecture are, and how this heritage is relevant today. Two illustrations guide the journey — a map showing where the chosen structures are located, and a timeline placing them in the river of time, running from Dholavira around 2600 BCE through the stupas, rock-cut caves, stepwells, temples, mosques, forts, tombs and havelis to twentieth-century Mysuru Palace.

The Beginnings …

This section returns to the Indus/Harappan/Indus-Sarasvati civilisation, presenting Harappan architecture as the earliest known tradition of urban planning and construction in the Indian subcontinent, marked by carefully laid-out cities, standardised urban concepts, and an emphasis on drainage, water management and civic organisation. Most Harappan cities were built of brick because they lay in alluvial plains where clay is abundant. Mohenjo-daro (in present-day Sindh, Pakistan) is shown through a densely inhabited area with wide streets and narrow lanes roughly oriented to the cardinal directions, homes with bathing areas, and an efficient drain network carrying used water out of the city; the plan of a large house suggests servants lived downstairs while owners lived upstairs, away from street dust and noise. Dholavira in the Rann of Kachchh is presented as another large, precisely planned Harappan city that made extensive use of locally available stone, its highest and most fortified area — the 'Castle' — showing smoothly finished stone blocks, a large sentinel room for screening people, and two highly polished segments of a stone pillar.

Structural Stupa Architecture

From the 4th or 3rd century BCE onward, the rise of Buddhism brought new architectural forms to north India that slowly spread to other regions, chief among them the stupa — a dome-shaped structure housing relics of the Buddha or revered monks — built in thousands from brick or stone. Larger stupas became complex ritual centres with railings, gateways and elaborate narrative panels, and at this early stage the Buddha was represented symbolically through footprints, the wheel of dharma, or the stupa itself rather than in human form. The main stupa at Sanchi in Madhya Pradesh, commissioned or patronised by Aśhoka, represents the earliest phase of monumental religious stone architecture in India: a worshipper entered through one of its massive toraṇas (ornamental gateways depicting urban and village life) and circumambulated the stupa within the enclosure of railings, the vedikā. Stupa worship is explicitly depicted at Bharhut, also in Madhya Pradesh, where richly carved stone railings bore numerous inscriptions recording donors' names; the section notes that stupas are common in Jainism too and that the form spread across India and beyond, and it carries a "Let's Remember" box pointing to Sanchi panels shown in earlier grades.

Rock-Cut Architecture

Rock-cut architecture is introduced as an efficient and durable mode of construction for religious communities, in which builders carved directly into rock to create caves for worship, residence or assembly, with complexes often evolving over centuries as religious needs and patronage changed. The Udayagiri–Khandagiri cave complex near Bhubaneswar in Odisha, begun in the 2nd century BCE under royal patronage, is among India's first and mostly served as dwellings for Jain monks; one cave shelters Khāravela's famous inscription. Maharashtra's complexes at Ajanta and Ellora are noted for their massive scale, exquisite craftsmanship and the technologies involved — many thousands of tonnes of rock had to be removed to leave the colossal monolithic Kailaśha Temple and the Buddhist, Hindu and Jain caves; Ellora's cave no. 10 holds a massive seated Buddha in a teaching pose leaning against a stupa, its arched roof imitating timber beams, with monks circumambulating the stupa in the aisle outside the row of pillars — the custom of pradakṣhiṇa, going around clockwise, which persists in later classical temples. The section contains a "Let's Explore" group discussion activity on carving such a cave with only hammers and chisels, a "Let's Remember" box on where these caves appear in earlier textbooks, and a "Don't Miss Out" box introducing four further sites: the Sharada Peetha temple in Jammu and Kashmir, a centre of learning tied to the Sharada script that flourished between the 6th and 12th centuries CE and gave Kashmir the name Śhāradā Deśh; the 16th-century Hidimba Devi Temple at Manali dedicated to Hidimbā of the Mahābhārata; the Jageshwar Group of about 124 stone temples in Uttarakhand linked to the Katyūrī, Chand and Gurjara–Pratihāra dynasties; and the Golden Temple or Harmandir Sahib in Punjab, holiest of Sikh shrines, founded by Guru Ramdas Sahib and continued by Guru Arjan Dev in 1588.

Water Structures

India's monsoon-driven climate led people over several millennia to develop a great variety of structures for harvesting, storing and distributing water, ranging from the humble village pond to sophisticated works such as the Grand Anicut, and differing widely with the geological, climatic and environmental conditions of regions from the Himalayan foothills and Thar Desert to the Gangetic plains, Western Ghats, Deccan plateau and Northeast. The tradition reaches back to the Harappans, seen in Dholavira's large reservoirs and in wells built from trapezoid-shaped bricks that lock together so the shaft cannot collapse inward under the pressure of underground infiltration. At Shringaverpur in Uttar Pradesh — the Śhringaverapura of the Rāmāyaṇa, where Rāma, Sītā and Lakṣhmaṇa crossed the Ganga — excavations revealed a series of large interconnected reservoirs fed by a channel from the Ganga during seasonal flooding, the first one or two allowing suspended silt to settle, though the working of the whole complex is still not fully understood. The section then describes the classical stepwell tradition of Gujarat and Rajasthan (vāvs or bāorīs), engineering marvels and works of art such as Rani ki Vav with its multiple pillared levels and hundreds of statues, which stored rainwater and also offered a cool social space; temple reservoirs or puṣhkariṇīs, used for water harvesting and water-based rituals and often built with numerous stairways; and the present-day threats to these structures from depleted sources, poor maintenance, pollution and waste dumping, against which environmentalists, locals and NGOs sometimes work together.

Classical Temple Architecture

Temples, with antecedents in stupa and rock-cut architecture, have been central to India's cultural life — Buddhist, Jain or Hindu places of worship that were also centres of learning, literature, art, social intercourse and economic life, evolving into works of art that celebrate life and encourage bhakti and quiet contemplation. Classical temples emerged in the Gupta era, built through royal patronage and donors' endowments and following the principles of śhilpaśhāstra, the discipline of arts and crafts devoted largely to architecture and sculpture; their main elements are the ceremonial entrance (gopuram in the south, toraṇa in some northern temples), one or several maṇḍapas or pillared halls, and the garbhagṛiha, the innermost chamber holding the mūrti of the deity, above which rises the śhikhara (north) or vimāna (south), with the main alignment generally east-west and the garbhagṛiha facing east. Philosophically, the temple is planned horizontally and vertically as a representation of the human body — that of Puruṣha, the cosmic being — from the top of the śhikhara or vimāna as the head down to the platform as the feet, so the temple symbolises both our own being and the cosmos, and worship becomes a journey from the outer world to the inner world of the garbhagṛiha. The section traces the growth of temples from modest size before the 8th or 9th century to vast complexes from the 10th century onward with multiple shrines, amenities and enclosures (prākāras), and visits four examples: the Bṛihadīśhvara Temple at Thanjavur built by Rājarāja Chola I around 1010, with vast maṇḍapas, a vimāna over 60 metres high, a massive single-stone Nandi, and intricate granite carvings; the early-11th-century Lingarāja Temple at Bhubaneswar, also dedicated to Śhiva with secondary shrines to Pārvati and other deities, built of local sandstone and laterite with three large maṇḍapas and ornate carvings; the Dilwara Temples at Mount Abu, five Jain temples of white Aravalli marble built between the 11th and 13th centuries by the Solanki or Chaulukya dynasty and covered in delicate carvings of tīrthankaras, deities, dancers and musicians; and the 17th–18th century terracotta temples of Bishnupur in West Bengal, brick structures with hand-carved, kiln-fired square panels depicting Rādhā, Kṛiṣhṇa, Rāma and everyday scenes. It closes by noting that such temples required not only large manpower and accomplished sculptors but highly skilled architects, engineers and craftsmen using elaborate techniques and a good deal of mathematics, especially advanced geometry — as was true of Egypt's pyramids, Athens' Parthenon and medieval Europe's Gothic cathedrals, with 'Gothic' defined in a margin note. The section carries student activities on the temple-as-cosmic-being's horizontal equivalence, on comparing regional temple styles, and on listing construction materials and identifying a local temple.

The Architecture of Mosques

Mosques are presented as places where followers of Islam gather for prayer and also as centres for religious education, community gatherings, lectures and discussions, with a prayer hall oriented towards Mecca and, in larger examples, one or several domes and minarets from which the muezzin calls to prayer; large mosques where the especially important Friday prayers are held are often called 'Jama Masjid', jama meaning gathering or assembly and masjid meaning mosque. The Jama Masjid in Ahmedabad, built by Sultan Ahmed Shah I in the 15th century, shows how architectural developments in Gujarat took on regional flavours: its yellow sandstone structure blends Hindu and Islamic elements, with elegant arches, the bases of two minarets whose tops were destroyed in earthquakes, intricate stone jālis or latticed screens such as the Sidi Saiyyed Jāli, and undecorated temple-style pillars that lead scholars to conclude local Hindu and Jain artisans, masons and stone carvers helped build it. This blending is called Indo-Islamic architecture, whose features include domes, arches both pointed and corbelled, jālis and floral motifs, with Rajasthani additions such as ornate jharokhās (overhanging balconies) and chhatrīs (pavilion domes); a margin note defines 'corbelled'. The Jama Masjid in Delhi, India's largest mosque, was built by Shah Jahan in the mid-17th century after he moved his capital from Agra to Shahjahanabad, using about 5,000 workers; it is made of red sandstone with white marble decorations, has three large entrances, two 40-metre minarets, three large domes over the main prayer hall, a courtyard holding up to 25,000 people, and walls and arches decorated with calligraphy of verses from the Qur'an, with the mihrab niche indicating the direction of Mecca.

Forts and Palaces

Forts are recalled from earlier chapters as structures that protected a kingdom from invaders, strategically located near important land or sea routes and often atop a hill to command a view of the plains, housing the residences of ruling and military classes and sometimes an entire city; because some forts also sheltered a royal palace, the chapter treats forts and palaces together, and a "Let's Remember" box asks students to recall the forts of Jaisalmer, Kumbhalgarh, Raigad and Sindhudurg and locate them on the map. Mehrangarh Fort in Rajasthan, begun in the mid-15th century and expanded by Rajput rulers over the next two centuries, overlooks Jodhpur and is built of massive blocks of locally sourced red sandstone, its palace containing royal areas, separate men's and women's quarters, staff quarters, stables and barracks, with much of it now a museum; a "Don't Miss Out" box explains the Jaswant Thada cenotaph and the reasons for Jodhpur's blue houses and cooling design features. The Red Fort at Agra, originally of brick and in ruins until Akbar rebuilt it as his capital, was designed with palaces, gardens, living quarters and halls for public and private audiences, takes its name from red sandstone mined at Dholpur some 60 km away, and was partly rebuilt in marble by Shah Jahan — who was imprisoned there until his death by his son Aurangzeb — with many buildings later destroyed by East India Company troops in the 19th century; a further box describes how Persian wheels lifted Yamuna water into overhead tanks to feed fountains that acted as eco-friendly 'air-conditioners'. The Orchha Fort-Palace complex, built in the mid-16th century by Bundela Rajput kings on the Betwa in Madhya Pradesh, contains the Raja Mahal, Jahangir Mahal and Rai Praveen Mahal, blending Rajput and Mughal styles with ornate chambers, stone carvings, courtyards and riverbank chhatrīs. Mysuru Palace (Amba Vilas Palace), residence of the Wadiyar dynasty, is an example of Indo-Saracenic architecture, rebuilt early in the 20th century after fire destroyed the wooden palace, and is known for carved wooden doors, ceiling paintings, stained glass, the golden throne displayed at Dasara, and its illumination by nearly 100,000 lights.

Tombs and Mausoleums

This section explains that in Islam and Christianity the dead are buried, and that in the Islamic world mausoleums — grand buildings housing one or more tombs — were built for prominent rulers, royal household members and renowned mystics, serving also as political statements to the greatness of a dynasty; in Buddhism, Jainism and Hinduism the dead are generally cremated, so no ancient tombs of rulers of those faiths exist in India, though medieval cenotaphs in Rajasthan and central India marked cremation places. These monuments embedded strict concepts of symmetry extending to their gardens, and shared features with larger mosques such as arches, floral motifs, latticework and Qur'anic calligraphy. Humayun's Tomb in Delhi was commissioned by his chief wife Bega Begum in 1558 and designed by Persian architects who brought Persian aesthetics into the Mughal architectural tradition; the complex also holds the tombs of several Mughals including Dara Shikoh, and a "Don't Miss Out" box explores the six-pointed star on the tomb as the Star of David in Judaism, the Seal of Solomon in Islam, and a tantric symbol of the fusion of puruṣha and prakṛiti in Hinduism. The Taj Mahal, built in the mid-17th century by Shah Jahan as a mausoleum for Mumtaz Mahal, is presented as a confluence of three cultures — Persian charbagh gardens and large arched doorways, Arabic Qur'anic calligraphy and geometric patterns, and Indian chhatrīs, lotus designs and marble screens — with delicate inlay work of semi-precious stones and white marble that appears to change colour through the day; a further box records the yellowing of the marble by industrial air pollution noticed in the 1970s and the mid-1990s ban that followed an expert committee's recommendations and a PIL in the Supreme Court.

Traditional Houses

House design in India has been as varied as its geography, and a common general design in traditional houses of the richer classes, urban and rural alike, is a central courtyard with rooms arranged around it over one or several storeys — a design archaeologists note was already used in some larger Harappan houses about 4,500 years ago; a "Let's Explore" activity asks students to work out its advantages in terms of privacy, convenience, rainfall and cooling. Havelis, the traditional mansions of Rajasthan's Shekhawati region, combine a central courtyard with elaborate decoration including intricate painted frescoes, were homes of the region's nobility and rich merchant class, and are today often converted into hotels or museums because they are hard to maintain or too large for modern families. The Chettinadu houses of Tamil Nadu belonged to a merchant community trading rice, spices and gems with Southeast Asia; their materials were sourced worldwide, including teakwood from Burma and marble from Italy, with craftsmen from across India working on the frescoes, and they are noted for being well adapted to the local climate, their central courtyard serving for rituals, community gatherings and sun-drying pāpads and chillies.

Churches in India

This short section recalls that some Christian communities have lived in India for many centuries and that, by tradition, the first churches were built in the coastal areas of Kerala. Over time different types of churches appeared, sometimes imitating classic European styles — as with St. Philomena's Cathedral in Mysuru, built in the Gothic style during the colonial period with the then Maharaja Krishnarajendra Wadiyar laying the foundation stone — and at other times, especially in Kerala, incorporating local architectural traditions. The examples given are the St. Thomas Orthodox church at Mulanthuruthy near Kochi, which has a stambha or flag post in front borrowed from temple architecture, and the Kalloopara church in Travancore, rebuilt in the 18th century with a sloped tiled roof typical of Kerala architecture and an interior with oil lamps and a traditional wooden roof.

Colonial Architecture

The final descriptive section introduces Indo-Saracenic architecture as a distinctive colonial style that flourished during the British Raj, from the mid-19th to the early 20th century. It blended Islamic, Hindu and Gothic elements, featuring domes, pointed arches, minarets and intricate decorative patterns inspired by Mughal and Rajput traditions. The examples illustrated are the Madras High Court, completed in 1892, whose red sandstone facade combines Islamic arches and intricate screens with carvings and pillars in Hindu temple style and is topped by domes and towers, and the Victoria Memorial Hall in Kolkata, now a museum, with its central arch and dome, balconies and chhatrīs.

Before we move on …

The closing recap consolidates the chapter's argument: India's architectural heritage is among the richest and most diverse in the world, and the selection made here necessarily leaves out thousands of astonishing monuments of diverse styles. It stresses that there is much thought and spirituality behind these bricks and stones — classical Buddhist, Jain and Hindu architectures seek to connect the human and the cosmic, or the human and the divine, aiming at beauty and order, and, as with Islamic architecture, involve advanced construction techniques. It ends on conservation: many lesser-known monuments are in a state of neglect, government bodies are theoretically responsible for the huge task of preserving thousands of centuries-old monuments, but much will be lost unless citizens feel equally responsible and learn to appreciate and admire this labour of our ancestors — a point tied to the Constitution's Fundamental Duty "… to value and preserve the rich heritage of our composite culture".
