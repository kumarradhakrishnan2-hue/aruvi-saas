Chapter 14: India's Urban Landscape

Introduction

The chapter opens with an epigraph from the American urbanist Jane Jacobs — that cities can provide something for everybody only when they are created by everybody — and four Big Questions: how cities become centres of diverse economic activities, in what ways urbanisation can impact people's lives, what challenges cities face in the twenty-first century, and how effective urban planning can improve life in cities. The introduction establishes that India has a long urban legacy running from Harappan cities such as Dholavira, Kalibangan and Rakhigarhi to historical centres such as Vijayanagara, Varanasi and Ujjain, most of which still flourish, and that Dholavira spread over 48 hectares with residential areas, fortifications, streets, complex water structures and community grounds. It then defines the urban as areas with a concentration of population, varied businesses and occupations, government institutions and elaborate transport and communication systems, governed by elected local bodies, and contrasts them with rural areas of villages with lower populations, close-knit communities, more agriculture-based occupations and gram panchayat governance. The section stresses that the definitions of urban and rural differ across the world — a place becomes urban above 220 people in Denmark, 50,000 in Japan and 5,000 in India — and a student activity asks learners to identify and describe their own settlement as village, town or city.

Population Density in Cities

This section introduces population density through the everyday image of a few friends in a room versus thirty people squeezed into the same space. It defines demographers as experts who study the characteristics of populations such as size, age structure and geographic distribution, and gives population density as the total population of a region divided by its area in square kilometres, usually expressed as people per square kilometre. A think-about-it activity uses satellite images of Asadpur Khera village in Jhajjhar and Gurugram city, both in Haryana, asking students to infer the density difference from the large green and brown open blocks in one and the dense grey built-up network in the other, and the term satellite view is defined as a top-down view of the Earth's surface captured by orbiting satellites. A chart of the world's most populous cities in 2025 accompanies the section, showing Jakarta at 4.19 crore, Dhaka at 3.66 crore and Tokyo at 3.34 crore, with two Indian cities — Kolkata at about 2.25 crore and New Delhi at about 3.02 crore — among them.

How do Cities Become Centres of Economic Activities?

This section explains historically why particular places grew into prominent urban centres: location near rivers gave ease of transportation, positions at the crossroads of trade routes allowed markets to develop, and access to agricultural produce and raw materials supported artisan communities and manufacturing, so such places became centres of economic activity as well as of political and administrative power. It supplies the scale of the shift with United Nations estimates that 68 per cent of the world's population will live in cities by 2050 compared with 55 per cent now, and notes the parallel Indian trend of urban population rising from 17 per cent in 1951 to around 40 per cent in 2025. Two student activities follow: comparing satellite images of Electronic City, Bengaluru from 2012 and 2025 to identify what changed and why, and a small-group project interviewing adults of different generations about how their own village, town or city has changed over the decades.

What Leads to Urbanisation?

This section traces the gradual transition from villages to small towns to megacities. A process diagram sets out the stages — rural origins in farm-related work, early urbanisation with a shift to secondary and tertiary sectors such as small-scale industries, transport and services and the emergence of small towns and trading centres, then the development of new infrastructure such as factories, hotels, government institutions, banks, entertainment and sports, a diversity of jobs, population growth from in-migration, and finally expansion into metropolitan areas with high-rise buildings, shopping malls, metro systems and busy transport networks. Urbanisation is defined as the population shift from rural to urban areas with a corresponding decrease in the proportion of people living in rural areas. Jamshedpur is the worked example: Sakchi, a small village on the Chota Nagpur Plateau, was chosen for Asia's first steel plant because of its proximity to rich iron ore deposits, the Tata family developed it as a township with modern facilities, Sir Dorabji Tata built a hospital before steel production began, and the village was renamed Jamshedpur in 1919, growing into Jharkhand's largest industrial city with wide tree-lined streets, gardens, open spaces and civic amenities. The section then generalises to geographical advantage — Mumbai on the Arabian Sea and Kolkata near the eastern coast became vital port cities — and a let's-remember activity asks students to recall why Surat became a textile and diamond-cutting hub. Further examples show how economic character develops: the Bombay Stock Exchange, set up in 1875, made Mumbai the financial capital, drawing talent and information flows that fed innovation and a film industry that began in the 1910s and flourished by the 1930s; Bengaluru, the 'Silicon Valley of India', became an IT hub through clusters of software firms, start-ups and higher education institutions linked closely to industry; and Hyderabad grew as a centre for technology-based enterprises. Alongside the large metropolises of Delhi, Mumbai, Bengaluru and Chennai, the chapter describes smaller specialised urban centres — Moradabad for brass work, Ludhiana and Tirupur for textiles, Jaipur for handicrafts and gem jewellery, Channapatna for wooden toys, Kanchipuram for silk sarees — and rapidly growing cities such as Kochi, Indore, Lucknow and Nagpur where lower operating costs attract warehouses and offices. Historical and cultural significance is a further driver, with Agra's Taj Mahal drawing 70–80 lakh visitors a year and pilgrim cities such as Amritsar and Varanasi generating livelihoods in transport, hospitality, local food vending and handicrafts. Delhi closes the section as a layered case: a centre of culture, trade and administration since the Iron Age, built up by Rajputs, Turks and Mughals and then by the British with a new capital, railways and electricity, and after Independence filled with ministries, industries, universities and research centres; population pressure on housing, water and sewerage was eased by creating the National Capital Region including NOIDA and Ghaziabad, supported by better roads, highways and the metro, while Gurugram has been a major IT and Business Process Outsourcing hub since the 1990s — BPO being defined as one company hiring another service provider to perform specific business functions at lower cost.

Cities as Cultural Hubs

This section argues that because cities draw people from many regions they create a potpourri of cultures, languages, customs, traditions, food and festivals that come to be shared along with shared spaces. Vaḍā pāv illustrates such fusion — a Maharashtrian-style potato dumpling in a pāv, with both bread and potatoes of Portuguese origin — an easy-to-make, easy-to-carry snack that became popular among workers in Mumbai's textile mills. Kolkata, once the capital of British-ruled India, retains institutions of that era such as the Indian Museum and Victoria Memorial while being famous for its five-day autumn Durga Puja, inscribed on UNESCO's list of Intangible Cultural Heritage of Humanity in 2021. The section also notes that cities allocate resources to cultural centres, libraries, museums, sports stadiums, open-air festivals and venues such as the Pradhanmantri Sangrahalay in New Delhi, creating spaces for national and international gatherings where inhabitants interact, build relationships and form networks. A student activity asks learners to name nearby towns and cities and find out traditions, cuisines and customs specific to them.

Challenges Faced by Cities

This section sets out the pressures that large populations place on urban resources, with rising demand for food, water, energy and housing straining infrastructure. It lists narrow and poorly maintained roads, rapid unplanned residential development with poor access to water, sanitation, energy and waste management, growing vehicle numbers, and rising temperatures from 'heat islands', along with mounting pollution and waste — the National Capital Region frequently records Air Quality Index levels above 400 against a safe limit of 50, and the AQI is defined as a single numerical scale combining pollutants such as PM2.5, PM10, carbon monoxide and sulfur dioxide. Higher crime rates in urban areas, linked to population density and economic inequality, are noted as requiring greater policing. Slums are defined as overcrowded settlements with poor housing and limited water, sanitation and municipal services that grow near city peripheries, railway tracks and industrial areas where vacant land exists, arising from a shortage of affordable housing for migrants; slum dwellers support cities through informal work such as vegetable vending, domestic help, laundry, construction and plumbing, and improving their housing, health and education infrastructure is identified as a key challenge, addressed by schemes such as the Pradhan Mantri Aawaas Yojana. A think-about-it activity accompanies a set of images of urban challenges — poor air quality, water scarcity, traffic jams, reduced green spaces, waste generation, monsoon flooding and slums — asking students what problems slum residents face.

Power of collective action in the revival of Bengaluru lakes

This case study examines water scarcity in Bengaluru, which faces summer shortages and monsoon flooding because of poor urban planning, groundwater overuse, lack of rainwater harvesting and encroachment of lake beds. The city once had around 1,000 lakes, reduced by rapid urbanisation to about 200, many polluted by sewage and chemicals, with wells drying up and lakes such as Bellandur and Varthur covered in foam, releasing toxic gases and even catching fire. Non-profit organisations, Resident Welfare Associations and local communities have revived lakes by desilting, repairing bunds with natural materials, clearing drainage channels, planting native species and restocking fish, as shown in the before-and-after images of Yelahanka Lake. Such collective action is presented as improving residents' quality of life by increasing water availability and maintaining ecological balance.

Planning Our Cities

This section argues that cities must be planned as dynamic systems with evolving needs, especially in view of climate change, and defines an urban planner as a person who plans and designs how land in cities and towns is used so as to optimise infrastructure and residents' quality of life. It frames planning as a set of questions: how many people live there and what work they do, what transport they need and how it can be interconnected for smoother commuting and universal access, where homes, businesses, parks and public facilities such as schools, hospitals, libraries, sports facilities and community centres should be located, and what public safety provisions are required, including safe streets for pedestrians and cyclists and accessible fire and police stations. The section also presents Indian urban redevelopment as requiring the integration of technology, citizen participation, efficient infrastructure and improved services in waste management, water supply, public transport, energy efficiency, housing and e-governance, illustrated by Ahmedabad's Sabarmati Riverfront with its promenades, parks, markets, boating and the pedestrian-and-cycle Atal Bridge, and by Gangtok's pedestrian-only Mahatma Gandhi Road, a clean and vibrant shopping hub. It closes by describing cities as living organisms that grow, adapt and transform, as centres of both consumption and production and spaces where traditions meet modernity, and by arguing that well-planned cities need innovative ICT such as intelligent traffic systems, digital surveillance, online grievance redressal platforms and solar-powered public spaces, while balancing growth against pollution, congestion, inequality and loss of green spaces so that opportunity does not exclude the vulnerable or exhaust resources.

India's 'Pink City'

This case study describes the planning of Jaipur, designed from 1727 by Vidyadhar Bhattacharya, chief architect and urban planner appointed by Maharaja Sawai Jai Singh II for his expertise in Indian architecture, vāstu shāstra and śhilpa shāstra. He laid the city out on a grid divided into rectangular blocks or chowkris aligned to the east-west and north-south cardinal directions, with wide streets, open spaces, gardens and nine public squares that served as centres of trade and interaction and held important buildings and temples, and enclosed the city with a twenty-foot wall and seven gates for security. Buildings along the major streets were built of local pink sandstone, giving a uniform appearance and a cohesive identity that earned the city its name, while the design allowed good sunlight, fresh air and a steady water supply through reservoirs and canals suited to the dry local climate, and markets were given shaded porches for traders and travellers. The section notes that Jaipur still works much as originally designed, a marvel of traditional planning, sustainability and aesthetics, and includes images of its pink sandstone buildings, the Panna Meena Kund stepwell and a 1914 map of the city.

Dynamic urban planning of Singapore

Singapore, an island nation with over 8,000 people per square kilometre, is presented as a model of urban planning that has grown while maintaining a high quality of life. Its planning instruments are a Concept Plan for land use looking up to forty years ahead and a Master Plan — defined as a long-term document providing a conceptual layout to guide future growth and development — updated every five years to keep planning flexible, which has carried the country from an overcrowded port in the 1960s to a modern city-state with strong transport, electricity, water supply and waste management infrastructure. The section describes mixed-use spaces where homes, offices and recreational areas lie close together, as with Marina Bay Sands and Raffles Place a few kilometres apart; a mass public transport system linking rail, buses, cycling paths and green walking corridors that reduces reliance on cars; and, to make use of limited land, sustainable high-rise buildings with rooftop and vertical gardens — vertical gardening being defined as growing plants on walls, fences or structures rather than on the ground — which earned Singapore the title of 'garden city' while reducing summer heat and supporting biodiversity. It further notes that Singapore's walkability and its integration of senior housing with healthcare, childcare, diverse neighbourhoods and green spaces make it a model for intergenerational living, and that its community-centred design places it among the blue zone cities, defined as places where people live significantly longer and healthier lives with low rates of chronic disease, as in Okinawa in Japan.

Before we move on …

The closing recap consolidates three points: cities are large human settlements that act as centres of diverse economic and cultural activities, and their growth results from factors such as locational advantage, the development of business clusters, tourism and efficient infrastructure; as cities grow they face challenges including air and water pollution, expanding slums, loss of green spaces, traffic jams and waste generation; and cities need to be planned dynamically and sustainably around the constantly changing needs of their inhabitants, so that with better planning they can remain centres of innovation, culture and economic prosperity.
