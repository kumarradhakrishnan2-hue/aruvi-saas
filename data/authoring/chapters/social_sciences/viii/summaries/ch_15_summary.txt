Chapter 15: Cultural Currents: 13th to 17th Centuries

Introduction

The chapter opens with a quotation from Mahatma Gandhi about letting the cultures of all lands blow freely about one's house without being blown off one's feet, and defines culture as the shared way of life passed down through generations — values, morals, customs and traditions, along with art, music, literature, philosophy and science. It argues that Indian culture is among the most ancient in the world and, unlike those of Mesopotamia, Egypt, Greece and Rome, has endured through political instability and invasion because of its deep roots, timeless values, scientific temper, and rich philosophical and artistic traditions, remaining open to adopting or adapting the best of external currents while also influencing the world. From ancient times India received merchants and traders, knowledge-seekers and scholars, diplomats and the persecuted — figures such as the Greek scholar Megasthenes, and Arab merchants who came for spices — from Greece, Persia, Central Asia and East Asia, all of whom brought their own social norms, cuisine, fashion, jewellery, art, language, literature, religion and festivals, and assimilated into Indian society. A new chapter began in the early 13th century, when rulers arrived with different political ideas, administrative systems, art and architectural styles, religious beliefs and worldviews; rather than attempt comprehensiveness, the chapter offers a few vignettes of cultural currents, continuity and change across visual art and architecture, music and dance, language and literature, science and mathematics, and philosophy, noting three common patterns — continuation of traditional practices, patronage from rulers of various backgrounds, and active participation by people across all sections of society — and the natural appearance of Persian and Arabic influences under Muslim patronage. The section is framed by three Big Questions: how Indian culture endured and grew richer despite invasions and instability, what the major cultural trends and movements were, and how the various currents interacted and mingled.

Belief Systems

This section sets up the religious tension of the period, contrasting a monotheistic Islam with one prophet against a religion with several schools of thought and no single holy book, and records that the desire of many invaders to annihilate the 'infidels', guided by their own version of Islam, led to the plunder and destruction of some of the oldest and most sacred sites and temples. It asks why, unlike other parts of the world where militant versions of a ruler's faith suppressed indigenous faiths, the belief systems native to India continued alongside Islam and other religions more than a millennium later. The question of what allowed multiple diverse groups to coexist in the subcontinent frames the subsections that follow.

The power of the bhakti tradition

This subsection explains the pre-existing cultural ground on which bhakti revived. Before the 13th-century invasions the Purāṇas and the two great epics were embedded in local culture through poetry, drama, puppetry, painting, dance and sculpture, and in daily life through temple visits and festivals, while the bhakti mārga (path of devotion) was established as one of several paths to mokṣha — realisation of Truth or release from the cycle of death and rebirth — requiring neither rituals nor priests. The tradition had crystallised by the 6th century, and its renewed popularity in this period reflected cultural continuity and society's search for spiritual resilience amid political and religious upheaval, with verses and songs composed in local languages expressing love, compassion and equality and connecting people through song, music and dance.

Regional saints and their messages

This subsection surveys the bhakti saints region by region. Kabirdas, presumably raised by a weaver couple, lived in the 15th century in present-day Uttar Pradesh and composed verses speaking of one god and decrying the rituals of both Hinduism and Islam; Ravidas, of the early 16th century and born into a family of leather-workers, wrote poetry promoting equality and the oneness of all, breaking down rigid walls around varṇa and jāti. Guru Nanak journeyed across regions spreading the message of one God and the equality of all people, his teachings preserved with the hymns of several Hindu saints in the Guru Granth Sāhib; other examples include Mirabai in Rajasthan, Chaitanya Mahaprabhu in the east, Tukaram, Dnyaneshwar and Namdev in the west, and Purandaradasa and Kanakadasa in the south. People from all sections of society joined gatherings where bhajans were sung with instruments and dancing, some reinforcing the oneness of god discussed in the Upaniṣhads — which connected with the one god espoused by Islam — and others building cultural unity and continuity. The subsection carries a student activity on a Kabir dohā comparing wheat ground into flour with the dissolving of differences between Ram and Rahim, and a reflective prompt on why music and dance mattered in spreading bhakti.

Sufism

Sufism is introduced as a path of Islam focused on devotion and an inner, personal search for god. Sufi saints sang devotional songs called qawwālīs that fused local classical musical styles with their own and carried powerful lyrics about love, faith and unity; Khwaja Moinuddin Chishti and Nizamuddin Auliya encouraged music as a way to connect with god, and the whirling dervish and zikr (remembrance of God through repetitive chanting or silent recitation of divine names) are defined as Sufi practices. Sufis also adopted yogic and Vedantic techniques — inner silence, asceticism, and meditation — to achieve spiritual union much like Vedantic paths to mokṣha, with Dara Shikoh championing the unity of these paths and facilitating the translation of some Upanishads into Persian, so that Bhakti and Sufi traditions often overlapped and poets enriched Indian literature with themes of unity and devotion; Rumi is quoted, and an image shows Guru Gobind Singh greeting a Sufi saint. The section closes by returning to Purandaradasa, the father of Carnatic music of the 15th–16th century, said to have given up his diamond business to become a devotee of Kṛiṣhṇa and to have composed as many as 4,75,000 kīrtanās before joining Krishnadevaraya's court, and by noting that in Tamil Nadu the legacy of the Ālvārs and Nāyanārs continued through the recitation of the Divya Prabandham and the Tiruvāchakam, devotional hymns to Viṣhṇu and Śhiva composed between the 6th and 9th centuries — evidence that embedded traditions stayed alive while new ideas were integrated, sustained less by royal patronage than by the participation of the people.

Musical Traditions — Continuation and Evolution

This section traces continuity and change in music and dance-drama, integral to Indian culture since ancient times with distinct local manifestations. The Nātyaśhāstra is described as formalising the traditions in an interdisciplinary way — bringing together dance, drama, music, science, mathematics, aesthetics and mythology — while remaining flexible with many regional streams; traditional storytelling accompanied by song, dance and drama varied by region, as with puppetry in Rajasthan and paṭṭachitra (paṭṭa meaning cloth, chitra meaning picture) in Odisha, West Bengal and Andhra Pradesh, whose themes came from the Rāmayaṇa, Mahābhārata and Purāṇas and were carried to village gatherings by travelling bards, with everyday emotions of love, grief, pain and longing also entering these traditions. Indian classical music developed in two related streams, Hindustani in the North and Carnatic in the South: in the late 15th century, under Raja Mansingh Tomar of Gwalior, himself an accomplished composer, dhrupad (dhruva meaning fixed, pada meaning verse) was developed to new heights, and Tansen (Rāmtanu Pandey) went to Akbar's court to become one of its nine ratnas, influenced by a Sufi saint while still composing in praise of Kṛiṣhṇa and Śhiva; Amīr Khusro, the Indo-Persian Sufi poet and singer of the mid-13th to early 14th century, is credited with developing the qawwālī by fusing Indian, Persian and Arabic singing traditions. Carnatic music continued to develop independently, largely unaffected by Persian and Arabic influences, while instruments such as the sitar and tabla evolved into modern forms through experimentation by makers and musicians of various backgrounds, drawing their repertoires from much older Indian instruments such as the pakhāwaj. A student activity invites learners to explore storytelling traditions in their own region.

From Walls to Paper — Miniature Paintings

This section explains how painting moved from cave art and the Ajanta murals into refined mural and fresco forms and then, after paper reached India in the 10th or 11th century, from walls to paper. The Rajput painting tradition thrived from the 15th to the late 19th century, depicting traditional Hindu themes and typical expressions of beauty — lotus flowers, banyan trees, elephants, cows and peacocks, women with large eyes, people of different skin colours — and Abul Fazl in the Ain-i-Akbari praised Hindu painters of the time. Mughal miniature painting flourished as a distinct genre shaped by Persian influences, depicting secular themes such as landscapes, court scenes and portraits; these court paintings reached their height in Akbar's atelier, where one artist drew the lines, another filled colours and a third added finishing touches, and miniatures also appeared in manuscripts including Persian translations of the Mahābhārata such as the Razmnama. The section stresses exchange between the genres in style, technique and colour — plants and gardens, animals, and court and hunting scenes entering Rajput painting according to patrons' taste, and Mughal court painters migrating to Bikaner to blend styles — even as traditional Hindu themes continued to flourish. A student activity asks learners to compare the miniatures shown for similarities, differences, changes and retentions.

Changing Landscapes — Gardens

Gardens are presented as an essential aspect of urban landscapes for millennia, with words such as udyāna, vātikā and nandana appearing in the Ṛigveda, Mahābhārata and Rāmāyaṇa, and gardens often built around temples with a pond or tank. With the arrival of the Mughals, gardens took a new dimension by borrowing the Persian charbagh design — four parts with gardens and waterways representing the rivers of Paradise mentioned in the Quran — with water channels, fountains and terraces, developed around forts, tombs and mosques, as at Nishat Bagh in Srinagar and the Mughal Gardens at Rashtrapathi Bhawan. Some 17th-century gardens such as those at Amer Fort, Jaipur borrowed Mughal elements, following geometrical patterns with water channels used aesthetically as well as for irrigation, and the section links this to the new architectural forms and techniques adopted by Indian artisans, noting that the evolution of localised forms shaped by patron taste, local traditions and materials, and craftsmen's expertise was a distinctive feature of the time. A group-project activity asks learners to identify local art and craft forms and how their knowledge and skills are transferred across generations.

Women in Society — Contextual Variations in Their Role and Status

This section argues that women's status varied by region and changed with political circumstance. It opens with the 10th-century Arab chronicler Abu Zaidu-l Hasan's observation that Indian princes allowed their women to be seen unveiled at court, then records that invasions and military campaigns from the 12th century onwards saw women and children imprisoned and sold as slaves in the markets of West and Central Asia, with some women committing jauhar; attempts to protect them reduced women's public appearance, and practices such as purdah (Persian for curtain or veil) and the ghūnghaṭ spread in parts of north India, sometimes draped only around the head, possibly as a symbol of nobility associated with the Muslim aristocracy, and largely confined to the north. Women in royal Muslim households were generally secluded, yet Razia Sultan, the first and only female ruler of the Delhi Sultanate in the first half of the 13th century, led military campaigns personally, outmanoeuvred conspirators, established educational institutions across Delhi, prioritised merit-based administration and appeared unveiled at court — acts that fuelled the conspiracy ending her brief reign — while in the early 17th century Nur Jahan, married to Jahangir, issued royal orders, attended court, had coins minted in her name and supported art, architecture and trade. In south India societal norms largely continued unchanged, with elite women exercising power, issuing grants and influencing political decisions as depicted in the wall engravings at Hampi; inscriptions record temple donations by queens and wealthy women, including entire villages, and community halls and wells built at women's initiative, leading the chapter to conclude that the period's economic prosperity could not have been reached without women's active participation. The section carries several student activities, including recalling other women from history who went against the grain, reading Fernão Nunes's account of the vocations of women in the Vijayanagar king's staff, drawing conclusions about these women's economic and social status, and imagining a 22nd-century historian writing about girls and women in 21st-century society.

Education and Knowledge Traditions

This section states that invasions and military campaigns disrupted India's educational landscape, yet the tradition of learning persisted from villages to temples, safeguarded and nurtured by kings, saints, poets and scholars, often against great odds. It serves as the frame for the two subsections that follow.

Institutions of learning

Education is shown to have thrived in gurukulas, maṭhas, temple schools, Buddhist vihāras and Jain pallis, where students lived with their teachers and studied Sanskrit and Prakrit grammar, astronomy, law, medicine and sacred texts, while villages had pāṭhaśhālās; in the South, Kanchipuram and Sringeri were renowned for advanced learning in philosophy and mathematics, and the Kerala School of Mathematics laid the seeds of infinite series and calculus. Under Muslim rule madrasās offered instruction in Arabic, Persian, Hindavi — an early version of the Hindi-Urdu language with Indian grammatical structure and integrated Persian and Arabic words — and Islamic studies, while the jāti and śhreṇi system for arts, craft and trade continued with its codes, norms and apprenticeships, these decentralised systems remaining largely unaffected by political turmoil except where whole cities were devastated. The Vijayanagara rulers were guided by pūrvamaryade, a Kannada term meaning the following of previously established practices of dharma and social norms, which sustained support for temples and mathās; the Italian traveller Pietro Della Valle wrote in 1623 of schools teaching reading, writing and arithmetic in coastal Karnataka, though schools then were more likely a priest's house or a temple maṇḍapa, as at Chennanna Basadi in Shravanabelagola. The destruction of large universities such as Nālandā and Vikramaśhilā during the late 12th-century invasions is described as an immense cultural loss that dispersed learned scholars and disrupted an entire ecosystem, and a student activity asks learners to consider its impact on education and knowledge in India and the world.

Vocational education and knowledge sharing

This subsection uses textiles to illustrate skill-based vocations with hands-on training from a young age and the integration of new materials and techniques. India's long tradition of weaving and regionally distinctive textiles is credited to ancient weaving communities collectively called the Koshtis, whose knowledge and skills passed through apprenticeships within a family or with a master in the guild; weavers from Gujarat moved to Banaras with Akbar's encouragement, and the Mughals introduced zarī or zardozī and brocades — rich fabric woven with a raised pattern, typically in gold or silver thread — which became part of the signature style of Banaras silk saris, with designs inspired by flora, fauna and geometric patterns. The subsection notes the Geographical Indication tag that gives Banaras-area weavers rights over their knowledge and restricts who may call their saris 'Banarasi', and the effort, supported by higher education institutions, to replace chemical dyes polluting the Ganga with vegetable dyes. It closes with pashmina shawls, woven from cashmere from the goats of Ladakh and Kashmir, which gained Mughal imperial patronage, became a symbol of nobility, were exported across Asia and demanded by fashionable Europeans, and carry embroidery of distinct Persian influence including the buteh or būtā (paisley) motif.

Growth of Literature and Language

This section describes flourishing literature across Sanskrit, Tamil, Telugu, Kannada, Odia, Hindavi, Bengali and Marathi in both everyday communication and poetic expression, illustrated through a few cases. Bhakti literature enriched the culture of writing through accessible poetry and song — Chaitanya Mahāprabhu influenced 16th-century Bengali literature, including biographies and a new style of folk song called Dhāmāli on Kṛiṣhṇa themes that also entered kathak performance, while the court-poet Kavindra Paramesvara translated the Mahābhārata into Bengali and Persian poetry was translated into Bengali, with secular themes emerging alongside. Patronage mattered: Krishnadevaraya patronised poets in the south and the Gajapati kings of Odisha fostered literary and spiritual thought, and Tirumalamba, a polymath and polyglot at the court of Achyutadevaraya, wrote the Sanskrit kāvya Varadambika Parinaya. As the language of the Mughals, Persian became the language of administration and courtly culture, producing histories and poetry that often preserved earlier Indian knowledge, and an influx of Persian scholars, poets and astronomers led to translations in both directions — three Persian translations of Bhāskarāchārya's Līlavatī, the first by Abu al-Faiz ibn Mubarak (Faizi), and a Persian version of Kalhaṇa's Rājatarangiṇī. A student activity asks learners to list words in their regional language that might have Persian roots.

The case of Dara Shikoh

This subsection profiles Dara Shikoh, Aurangzeb's older brother in the 17th century, as an advocate of Hindu-Muslim harmony often seen in the company of yogis, pandits and Muslim scholars. A remarkable scholar himself, he is credited with translating 52 Upaniṣhads from Sanskrit into Persian with the help of learned scholars in Varanasi, translations that later reached Europe and influenced leading philosophers and thinkers in the 19th century. He was persecuted by Aurangzeb, who captured him, accused him of heresy and executed him.

The development of Urdu

Urdu is described as developing in the Ganga-Yamuna plains from the Hindi/Hindavi spoken there, integrating words from Arabic and Persian; it replaced Persian as an official language of the British government in India and is listed in the Eighth Schedule of the Constitution. South of the Deccan, Urdu becomes Dakhini by taking loan-words from local languages such as Telugu and Marathi, so a traveller from Lucknow to Hyderabad may find the Urdu there unfamiliar. Amir Khusrau is named the father of Urdu literature: writing primarily in Hindavi with extensive knowledge of Sanskrit, he also wrote regularly in Punjabi and Braj Bhasha, and the creative mixing of expressions across languages in his and others' writing has enriched Indian culture and entered popular culture through films and renowned singers.

Developments in Science and Technology

This section justifies including science in a culture chapter by pointing to India's long culture of enquiry, questioning, experimentation and learning, and to the spirit of letting noble thoughts come from all directions. It records that scientific and technological traditions continued to advance with support from regional courts and temple institutions: the production of pure zinc reached its peak using a special distillation process employed for the first time in the world, major texts of chemistry and medicine were composed, and in the 14th century Narayana Pandita authored the Gaṇitakaumudī, a massive mathematical treatise containing original developments. In the South at about the same time Madhava made pioneering contributions to calculus, including the series expansion for trigonometric functions and an exact value of π as an infinite series, both world firsts; he and his followers form the Kerala School of Astronomy and Mathematics, which produced important works until the 19th century, and in 1500 Nilakantha Somayaji worked out a planetary model in which Mercury and Venus orbited the Sun in an orbit not enclosing the Earth, decades before Copernicus's heliocentric model of 1543. Applied chemistry produced refrigeration and new perfumes such as the rose-based Itr Jahangir, Indian saltpetre (potassium nitrate) was used in Akbar's time for gunpowder and for cooling water and beverages, and the medical systems of Ayurveda, Siddha and Unani were widely practised with knowledge transmitted through texts, oral tradition and palm-leaf manuscripts.

Regional Architectural Developments

This section states that between the 13th and 17th centuries Indian art and architecture reached remarkable heights, blending tradition with innovation across regions and dynasties, from the intricately carved temples of the South to the majestic forts and palaces of the North, each structure reflecting the values, beliefs and aesthetics of its time. Patronage from kings, queens and nobles fostered a thriving artistic environment and iconic monuments, illustrated by the stone wheels of the 13th-century Sūrya Mandir at Konark built under the Eastern Ganga dynasty, the 15th-century Ranakpur temple in Rajasthan dedicated to Tīrthankara Ṛiṣhabhanātha and built by the Jain businessman Dhanna Seth, the Sheesh Mahal built by Raja Man Singh I at Amer Fort with tiny convex mirrors inlaid in walls and ceilings, and the ceiling of the mosque at the Taj Mahal. The section cross-refers to the chapter 'A Journey Through Indian Architecture' for fuller detail.

Temple building and reconstruction

Under Krishnadevaraya's rule the Vijayanagara Empire is described as witnessing a golden age of art, architecture and cultural revival, with Hampi a vibrant centre of urban planning and temple building where grand temples were constructed with remarkable skill, devotion and engineering knowledge, reinforcing religious devotion while testifying to the empire's prosperity and cultural vitality. The subsection stresses that these developments took place while wars or the threat of war were present, and that resilience and spirit are best exhibited by the reconstruction of destroyed temples and the reinstatement of sacred vigrahas. The Sri Ranganathaswamy Temple at Srirangam, ravaged in the early 14th century and rebuilt by the end of the same century with further temples added later, is given as the example of such restoration.

Cultural Exchange in Daily Life

Food

This subsection shows two-way influence in cuisine and culinary practice, particularly among the ruling classes. Spices such as cardamom, clove, turmeric, coriander and black pepper entered imperial kitchens and then common households, the use of curd for marination and of ghee became popular, and khichri — a lentil-and-rice dish made for over a millennium — took on new flavours; Akbar is said to have tried to reduce meat consumption for himself and his court, partly under the influence of Jain and Hindu traditions. Persian influence is traced in naan cooked in a clay oven, in the samosa as the Indian adaptation of the Persian sambūseh brought to the Delhi Sultanate courts by chefs from West Asia with its meat, pistachio, almond and walnut stuffing replaced by potatoes and peas, and in jalebi as the Indian version of zalabiyeh, with circular rather than asymmetric floral coils and simple sugar syrup instead of honey and rose water. A student activity asks learners to list popular dishes in their region and explore their origins and cross-cultural influences.

Clothing

This subsection notes that Indians have been conscious of attire since ancient times, with Greek travellers recording fine clothing, dyes, hairstyles, footwear including high heels, and jewellery, making experimentation with newly encountered styles natural, especially among the ruling class. Cotton, muslin and silk production rose during this period; the jama, a tunic-like dress, and the pyjama were adopted at court with variations for women, fine muslin sari-inspired dupattas added to the style, and the sari with its rich regional variations continued in the wardrobe. The angrakha, a robe-like upper garment worn by both men and women, is believed to have evolved from Persian court fashion and been adapted to Indian aesthetics and fabrics, while paintings of Shah Jahan's nobles illustrate turbans and vibrant colours. A student activity asks for observations about continuity and change in clothing styles today and the reasons behind them.

Before we move on …

The closing recap consolidates the chapter's argument: cultural continuity as well as integration occurred in everyday aspects from art, music and languages to clothing and food; the bhakti tradition became stronger and served as a unifying force during political instability, its saints promoting equality and the oneness of god and breaking down rigid social barriers, while Sufi saints formed a valuable bridge between cultures through unity, devotion and music. It restates that Indian music evolved while keeping its core identity in the two related streams of Hindustani and Carnatic classical music, that miniature painting demonstrated exchanges between Rajput and Mughal styles in technique, colour and compositional elements, that architecture blended traditions to create new styles, and that temple construction continued and destroyed temples were rebuilt as a demonstration of cultural resilience. It ends by noting that despite the destruction of major universities, education continued through decentralised systems, that the period saw groundbreaking contributions to mathematics and particularly calculus, and that scholars translated texts between Sanskrit and Persian.
