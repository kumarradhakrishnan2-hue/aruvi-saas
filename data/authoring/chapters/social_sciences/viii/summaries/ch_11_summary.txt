Chapter 11: The Role of the Judiciary in Our Society

Introduction

This chapter introduces the judiciary as the branch of the State that ensures fair adjudication of disputes in accordance with the law, opening with Dr Martin Luther King Jr's line that injustice anywhere is a threat to justice everywhere and with the Supreme Court of India in New Delhi. It is framed by four Big Questions: what the concept of justice is, why justice is important for a just and harmonious society, what the structure and role of the judiciary in India is, and what the alternative modes of dispute resolution are. The Preamble to the Constitution of India is used to establish that all Indian citizens are promised social, economic, and political justice, and that the founders of independent India laid the foundation for a sound judicial system in a democratic society; justice is presented as essential for peace and harmony and for individuals to live with equality and dignity. A student activity asks learners to recall a time they felt someone had been unfair to them and to discuss in small groups what would have made the situation right, and a recall box restates that the judiciary is one of the three pillars of government, independent of the legislature and the executive, and that this independence is essential for disputes to be decided fairly. The section closes by noting that disputes arise from diverse personal and professional contexts and are often resolved through dialogue, mediation, or adjudication by courts of law, and that the judiciary ensures laws passed by the legislature stay within the framework of the Constitution and that the executive does not overstep its constitutional role.

The Connection Between Law and Justice

This section explains how justice came to be administered through law as human societies grew larger and more complex and systems of governance emerged, with governments given the power to administer society through laws and to enforce them, with force if needed. Laws are defined as general rules made by the government to regulate individuals' activities and to impose consequences for not following them — a fine, compensation to the affected party, or in some cases imprisonment — and the system that enables a person to complain about a grievance and to place their point of view so that justice is ensured within the framework of the law and the Constitution is called the judicial system. The section then shows that laws are not automatically just: post-independence, hundreds of colonial-era laws were found discriminatory and contrary to the fundamental principles of a democratic polity, and over 1500 such laws have been repealed, including the Criminal Tribes Act, 1871, which labelled certain communities as criminals by birth, and the Vernacular Press Act, 1878, which restricted freedom of the press. Comparable unjust laws elsewhere are cited — the 'Jim Crow laws' in parts of the United States, which barred African Americans and other people of colour from water fountains, public pools, and restaurants reserved for white people and were officially repealed in the 1960s, and the apartheid-era laws of South Africa that divided people by race and were repealed in 1991. Returning to the opening activity, the section draws out two important aspects of justice: substantive justice, which focuses on the final result or outcome and asks whether the decision is actually fair, just, and reasonable, and procedural justice, which means every person involved gets a full opportunity of hearing before the court decides, under the rule that every person is presumed innocent until proved guilty.

The Judicial System in India

This short section sets out the architecture of the Indian judiciary as a unified system comprising a hierarchy of courts. The Supreme Court of India sits at the apex level, the High Courts at the State level, and the Subordinate Courts at the District level. A simplified diagram illustrates this three-tier hierarchy of Supreme Court, High Courts, and District Courts.

The Supreme Court of India

The Supreme Court is introduced as the highest court of law in the country, which came into existence on 26.01.1950, the date the Constitution of India came into effect, and is the final interpreter of the Constitution. Article 124 provides for its establishment and it is seated in New Delhi; its current strength is 33+1, that is the Chief Justice of India — the designation given to the Chief Justice of the Supreme Court — and 33 judges, all appointed by the President of India under Article 124. The qualifications for appointment require that the person be a citizen of India and additionally have been a Judge of a High Court or High Courts for five years, or an Advocate of one or more High Courts for ten years, or a distinguished jurist in the opinion of the President; a Supreme Court judge retires at the age of 65. A 'Don't Miss Out' box draws attention to the Supreme Court's logo and its Sanskrit motto, 'yato dharmastato jayah' — 'Where there is dharma, there is victory' — recalled from the Grade 6 textbook.

Writ Jurisdiction of the Supreme Court and High Court

This section explains that writ jurisdiction has been conferred on the Constitutional Courts for the enforcement of the Fundamental Rights guaranteed by Part III of the Constitution, rights that include Equality, Liberty, Right to Life, Freedom of Speech, and Freedom of Religion. For their enforcement a citizen has the constitutional right to file a "Writ Petition" directly before the Supreme Court under Article 32 where the issue has an all-India ramification, or before the High Court under Article 226 where the violation concerns the Fundamental Rights of citizens within the territorial limits of the State.

Public Interest Litigation (PIL) filed under Articles 32 and 226

PIL is presented as an innovation introduced by the Supreme Court to address issues of public concern and to give meaning to the Right to Life under Article 21. The first reported PIL case is described: a complaint by a lawyer, Mrs. Kapila Hingorani, who had read that under-trial prisoners in Bihar had been in jail longer than the punishment they would have received if found guilty; she filed a writ petition to secure their release, and the Supreme Court immediately ordered the release of such prisoners and issued guidelines leading to the release of almost 40,000 under-trial prisoners across India. Two further important PIL cases are given: M.C. Mehta v. Union of India, through which environmental issues such as the cleaning of the River Ganga and the protection of the Taj Mahal were taken up by the Supreme Court, and Vishaka & Ors. v. State of Rajasthan, in which the Supreme Court laid down guidelines against sexual harassment at the workplace in 1997, followed in 2013 by the Government's enactment of the Sexual Harassment of Women at Workplace (Prevention, Prohibition and Redressal) Act, 2013.

Original Jurisdiction of the Supreme Court

This section sets out the Supreme Court's exclusive original jurisdiction under Article 131 to decide legal disputes between the Centre and a State, between the Centre and a State on one side and another State on the other, and inter-state disputes such as river water disputes.

Appellate Jurisdiction of the Supreme Court

The Supreme Court can hear an appeal from any final order of a High Court if the High Court itself certifies that the case involves interpretation of the Constitution or a substantial question of law, under Articles 132, 133 and 134A. Judgments of the High Court in criminal cases involving a death sentence, or where a judgment is certified as fit for appeal, can be heard by the Supreme Court under Articles 134 and 134A. The Supreme Court also has jurisdiction to hear appeals from specialised tribunals such as the National Green Tribunal (NGT) and the National Company Law Appellate Tribunal (NCLAT).

Advisory Jurisdiction

This section explains that if the President of India seeks the opinion of the Supreme Court on a question of law or fact of public importance, the Court can give its opinion to the President. Such cases must be heard by a 'Constitution Bench' of not less than five judges, under Article 143.

Special Leave Petitions (Article 136)

The Supreme Court has an extraordinary discretionary jurisdiction to grant 'Special Leave to Appeal' from any judgment, decree or order made by any court or tribunal in the territory of India, where a question of law of general public importance has arisen or where it is needed to do substantive justice between the parties. The section adds that the law declared by the Supreme Court is binding on all courts within the territory of India under Article 141, and a 'Don't Miss Out' box notes the Court's power to pass any order necessary for doing complete justice under Article 142 and the requirement under Article 144 that all civil and judicial authorities act in aid of the Supreme Court.

High Court

A High Court is the highest court at the State level, and Article 214 provides that there shall be a High Court in each State; India has 28 States but 25 High Courts, since in some cases two or more States or Union Territories share a common High Court. A table lists all 25 High Courts against the States and Union Territories they cover — for instance the Bombay High Court covering Maharashtra, Goa, and the Union Territory of Dadra & Nagar Haveli and Daman & Diu, the Calcutta High Court covering West Bengal and the Andaman & Nicobar Islands, the Gauhati High Court covering Assam, Nagaland, Mizoram and Arunachal Pradesh, and the Madras High Court covering Tamil Nadu and Puducherry — and a student activity asks learners to find out, with their teachers' help, which High Courts have more than one bench. Each High Court consists of a Chief Justice and other judges, the number varying from State to State, and the judges are appointed by the President of India in consultation with the Chief Justice of India, the Governor of the State, and the Chief Justice of the concerned High Court. Under Article 217(2) a High Court judge must be a citizen of India and must have held a judicial office in India or been an advocate of one or more High Courts for ten years; High Court judges retire at sixty-two under Article 217(1) and 224(3).

Jurisdiction of the High Courts

Each High Court exercises its powers within its territorial limits and exercises writ jurisdiction under Article 226. It entertains appeals against decisions of subordinate courts in civil and criminal matters as well as decisions of certain tribunals, and exercises supervisory control over the functioning of all subordinate courts within its territorial limits. Every High Court also has the power of superintendence over all courts and tribunals within its territorial jurisdiction under Article 227 — a supervisory power exercised sparingly, to ensure that subordinate courts and tribunals act within the bounds of their authority. The section notes that the writ and supervisory jurisdiction of the High Courts has been declared to be part of the basic structure of the Constitution.

Subordinate Courts

In each State there is a system of subordinate courts below the High Courts, with Articles 233 to 237 of the Constitution regulating their organisation and ensuring the independence of the subordinate judiciary. The subordinate judiciary is described as a very important segment of the judicial system because it is in these courts that the judiciary comes into close contact with the people; it broadly includes District Courts, Sessions Courts, Revenue Courts, and Small Causes Courts. Each High Court is required to maintain and uphold the honour and integrity of the subordinate judiciary in its State, and the Governor of a State appoints the District Judges in consultation with the High Court under Article 233. A short student activity asks learners which subordinate court is closest to where they live.

Tribunals

This section introduces specialised tribunals established under various enactments, which are quasi-judicial in nature and decide specific categories of disputes; a margin note defines a tribunal as a committee or body established to settle certain types of disputes. A diagram maps six tribunals to their jurisdictions: the National Company Law Tribunal (NCLT) for corporate civil commercial disputes including insolvency and bankruptcy, Consumer Fora for consumer disputes, the Appellate Tribunal for Electricity (APTEL) for electricity disputes, the National Green Tribunal for environmental issues, the Armed Forces Tribunal for disputes related to the Armed Forces, and the Central Administrative Tribunal for service matters of government employees. A student activity presents a real consumer case in which a family that had paid an advance of almost ₹65 lakhs for a flat found no construction four years later and approached the National Consumer Commission, which ordered the builder to return the amount with interest and to pay the cost of the litigation. A 'Don't Miss Out' box records that the Indian Penal Code, 1860 has been replaced by the Bhartiya Nyaya Sanhita, 2023, the Code of Criminal Procedure, 1973 by the Bharatiya Nagarik Suraksha Sanhita, 2023, and the Indian Evidence Act, 1872 by the Bharatiya Sakshya Adhiniyam, 2023, laws that aim at faster justice, use of digital evidence, and better protection for victims.

Digital Technology and Judicial System

This section explains that digital technology has been introduced to make the judicial system more accessible and transparent, for example by allowing litigants to check the details of their own cases directly instead of relying only on their lawyers, and by making copies of court orders and judgments freely available to the public in multiple languages so that anyone can read and understand the proceedings. A margin note defines a litigant as a person involved in a legal case — both the person who sues and the person who gets sued — and the section notes that case details are available on each court's website and through a portal called the National Judicial Data Grid of India. A 'Don't Miss Out' box lists digital initiatives making the system more user-friendly: e-Filing, hybrid hearings, live streaming of court proceedings, translation of judgments into multiple Indian languages, and the National Judicial Data Grid. It also records that since 2024-2025 a large display screen dedicated to a 'Virtual Justice Clock' has been positioned at the main entrance gates of the Supreme Court of India, reinforcing commitment to transparency, accountability, and free access to real-time judicial data.

Alternate Modes of Dispute Resolution

This section explains that to strengthen the system and reduce the burden on the judiciary, courts increasingly encourage litigants to settle disputes through alternate modes such as arbitration, mediation or conciliation, negotiation, and Lok Adalat. Arbitration is defined as a mode in which parties agree to refer their disputes to an independent adjudicator or panel called an arbitrator — who may be a retired judge, a lawyer, or an expert — who must act fairly and impartially and allow each party to put forth its case; the decision is final, legally binding, and enforceable by a court of law, and arbitration in India is conducted under the Arbitration and Conciliation Act, 1996. Mediation, the most common form of alternate dispute resolution, involves a neutral Mediator who assists the disputing parties in arriving at a mutual settlement but has no power to make a decision or impose a view, and is governed by The Mediation Act, 2023. Lok Adalats are special fora where disputes and cases pending in courts can be settled amicably, and which parties may in some cases approach even before going to court; rooted in the principle of access to justice and given statutory recognition under the Legal Services Authorities Act, 1987, they function as people's courts that reduce judicial backlog without the rigours of formal adjudication. Gram Nyayalayas, established under the Gram Nyayalayas Act, 2008, decentralise the judicial system and reduce the burden on regular courts by functioning as mobile, village-level courts that adjudicate disputes through a simplified procedure with no technicalities.

Before we move on…

The closing recap consolidates the chapter's argument: the judiciary is one of the three pillars of the government; the key roles of the judicial system are to ensure that justice is served and that laws are made within the framework of the Constitution; the Constitution prescribes separation of powers among the legislature, executive and judiciary, and requires the judiciary to remain independent of the other organs of government; the judicial system addresses a huge number of cases on a regular basis, resolved at different levels of courts as well as through alternative modes of dispute resolution; and citizens have a role in helping the judicial system by bringing to its notice social concerns that affect the rights of people, animals, and nature.
