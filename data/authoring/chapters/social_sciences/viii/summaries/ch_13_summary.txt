Chapter 13: Dynamics of Population

Introduction

This chapter introduces students to the study of human population — its size, structure, and the forces that change it over time — and connects those numbers to the way societies and economies are shaped. It opens with a quotation from Ban Ki-moon, former Secretary-General of the United Nations, that population ageing is poised to become one of the most significant social transformations of the twenty-first century, with implications for nearly all sectors of society. The chapter is framed by four Big Questions: what demography is, what its important concepts are, how the population of India and the world has changed over time, and how population trends shape societies and economies. It sits within the textbook's theme of Economic Life Around Us, and moves from definitions and indicators, through the stages of demographic transition, to India's own composition, trends, and demographic dividend.

What Is Demography?

This section defines demography as the study of the features of the human population — its composition in terms of factors like age, sex, occupation, and religion — and how these change over time through births, deaths, and the movement of people within and across countries. It explains that the human population is spread around the world and differs in size, structure, and composition, with people of different age groups, genders, education levels, occupations, and religions; the three components of demography are presented as size, structure and composition, and pattern and trend. Key terms introduced here are structure of population (how a population is made up on the basis of characteristics such as age, gender, and education) and working-age population (individuals between the ages of 15 and 64 years, generally considered the most productive in terms of economic output and workforce participation). The section explains why this knowledge matters: governments use it to plan infrastructure such as schools, hospitals, roads, and housing, and municipal services like water, energy, and waste management — more schools where there are many young children, more healthcare facilities where the elderly form a larger share, and more employment generation where the working-age population is large — while businesses use age, gender, and income to design products, so that knowing a country's population size and structure helps it allocate and manage resources. A "Don't Miss Out" note explains the census as the official count of people living in a country at a particular time, conducted in India generally every ten years, gathering information on age, gender, marital status, education, languages spoken, religion, family size, occupation, employment status, and income.

Population at a Glance

This section presents the scale and trajectory of population growth in India and the world. It records that in 2023 India surpassed China to become the world's most populous country, with Indians now constituting around 17 per cent of the world population, and shows a bar graph of the most populous countries in 2024 — India at 144 crore and China at 140 crore, followed by the United States, Indonesia, Pakistan, Nigeria, Brazil, Bangladesh, Russia, and Mexico. World population grew from 2.5 billion in 1950 to 8.2 billion in 2025 and is expected to reach 9.7 billion by 2050, largely because improved healthcare has enabled people to live longer; growth has nonetheless slowed, and United Nations projections place the peak at 10.3 billion by the mid-2080s, after which the population will gradually decline as birth rates fall. The section stresses that growth varies across nations — China, Japan, and Italy are already seeing shrinking populations, India's is still growing, and African nations such as Nigeria and Ethiopia continue to grow rapidly — and supports this with graphs of world population trends (1951–2100), India's growth trajectory from 361 million in 1951 to 1,460 million in 2025, and the regional distribution of the 8.2 billion world population in 2025 across Asia, Africa, Europe, Northern America, Latin America and the Caribbean, and Australia and Oceania. Terms defined alongside include the United Nations as an intergovernmental organisation and projection as an estimate or forecast of a future situation based on present trends. A second "Don't Miss Out" note explains the rapid growth from the 1950s onwards through medical advances such as antibiotics, vaccines, and oral rehydration solution, better water and sanitation, falling child deaths from malaria and diarrhoea, and the spread of economic prosperity. A student exploration activity asks learners to ask family members about changes they have observed, such as farmland turning into housing or increasing traffic congestion.

What Determines the Population of a Country?

This section explains that a country's present population is the result of years of gradual change in the number of babies born, the number of deaths, and the movement of people in and out of the country. It introduces the four key indicators of demography: birth rate, the number of live human births per 1,000 population in a year; fertility rate, the average number of children a woman would have in her lifetime; death or mortality rate, the number of deaths per 1,000 people in a year; and migration, the movement of people from one place to another, within or outside the country.

Birth and fertility rates

This subsection explains how the two rates work and how they differ. A high birth rate means the population will grow faster, while a low birth rate means it may grow slowly or begin to shrink. A fertility rate of 2.1 is called the replacement level fertility rate because it is the number of children needed to replace the parents in the population and keep it stable — above 2.1 the population is likely to grow, below 2.1 it may shrink over time. The subsection shows that the two are closely linked: when fertility falls, birth rates drop and growth slows, and sustained low fertility can stop or reverse growth. A "Don't Miss Out" case study covers China's One-Child Policy, introduced in 1979 out of fears of shortages of food, jobs, and housing and enforced through fines and job loss; China's fertility rate fell from 7.5 in 1963 to 1 in 2025, its population peaked at 1.43 billion in 2022 and is projected to fall to 1.31 billion by 2050, the elderly share is expected to rise from 14 per cent in 2023 to 26 per cent in 2050, and a whole generation grew up without siblings and kinship networks (an interconnected web of social relationships through blood, marriage, and adoption); though the policy ended in 2015 and three children are now allowed, fertility remains below replacement level. India's parallel is the 'Hum do humare do' campaign from 1952 onwards, with India's fertility rate falling from 5.7 in 1950 to 1.9 in 2025, and a graph comparing fertility rates in India, Japan, Spain, and China from 2000 to 2023 alongside a list of other low-fertility countries. Falling fertility and birth rates are attributed to better access to quality education, especially for girls, later marriage and formal employment for women, and the high cost of living under rapid urbanisation, which makes people prefer smaller families. Two student activities appear here: one asks why the replacement rate is 2.1 rather than 2, and another asks learners to examine a table of fertility and female literacy rates in selected Indian states and prepare a report on the relationship between the two indicators.

Death or mortality rate

This subsection presents the death rate as another important indicator of population growth, noting that a lower mortality rate indicates the availability of quality healthcare and better nutrition. India's progress is illustrated through child mortality — the number of deaths of children under five per 1,000 live births, a key indicator of child survival and overall child health — which fell from 271 per 1,000 in 1950 to 28 in 2023. Life expectancy at birth in India rose from just 30 years at independence to 70 years in 2024, and the subsection carefully explains that life expectancy is an average for a newborn under current health, safety, and living conditions, not a prediction for every individual.

Migration

This subsection explains that people migrate for better employment opportunities and education, and that natural disasters and political instability can push people to move. Migration is defined as movement from one place to another, within or outside a country, to settle temporarily or permanently, and areas receiving an influx of migrants see their population rise — Delhi, Mumbai, and Bengaluru are given as Indian cities that have grown significantly this way. The subsection also flags the cost: concentrating a very large population in a limited area strains resources such as water, air, and land and services such as transportation and housing, a theme carried into the next chapter. A "Think About It" activity asks what changes could occur in native villages when many young people migrate to cities.

How Do Countries Experience Population Change?

This section introduces the theory of demographic transition by analogy with the phases of an individual life, explaining that a country's demographic characteristics change over time along with its birth and death rates, influenced by economic development, improved healthcare, education, and greater participation of women in household decision-making. Three stages are described and illustrated on a graph of birth rate, death rate, and population growth over time. In Stage 1 both birth and death rates are high, so growth is very slow or stagnant — as in India from 1901 to 1921, when healthcare was poor and literacy low. In Stage 2 the death rate falls with better healthcare while birth rates stay high, causing rapid growth — as in India after 1951. In Stage 3 death rates remain low and birth rates begin to fall because of rising urbanisation, higher incomes, education, and more women in the workforce having fewer children; if fertility drops sharply the population begins to shrink, as in European and East Asian countries such as Italy, Japan, and South Korea, while in India fertility began declining after the mid-1980s and is now below replacement level.

India's Population: Composition and Trends

This section turns to India's own population, describing it as diverse and dynamic and spread unevenly across states, with a chart showing the percentage share of population by state — Uttar Pradesh at 16 per cent, Maharashtra, Bihar, and West Bengal next, down to states and union territories with less than 1 per cent. Beyond geographical distribution, it explains that characteristics such as age, gender, occupation, and education make up the structure of the population, also called population composition. The section then introduces the population structure diagram as a tool for studying composition and change over time: a special graph showing the number of people by age group and gender, with young people at the bottom, adults in the middle, and the elderly at the top. A wide base means many children are being born and the country has a young, growing population, as in India or Nigeria; a widest middle means most people are working adults and the population is stable; a wide top means an ageing population, as in Japan. A "Don't Miss Out" note observes that although the term 'population pyramid' is widely used, the representation on paper looks more like a triangle whose parts have been pulled outwards or shrunk — a three-dimensional concept shown in two dimensions. Comparing India's pyramids from 1950 to 2030, the section notes that in the 1950s the shape was a narrow triangle with many children and few older people, a high birth rate, and low life expectancy of around 30–35 years, whereas in recent years the pyramid has become more bell-shaped: a narrower base as families have fewer children, a much wider middle showing a large 15–60 working-age group known as India's demographic dividend, and a slowly widening top as people live longer — a movement from a young, fast-growing population to a more balanced one that may begin to age like Japan or China. A "Think About It" activity asks what the pyramid would look like if the replacement rate fell below 2 and what would happen to such a country's population over time.

Improvement in India's sex ratio

This subsection introduces sex ratio as a key demographic indicator of population composition, defined as the number of females per 1,000 males and used to measure gender balance while reflecting the country's socio-economic and cultural dynamics. It records that India's sex ratio stood at 933 females per 1,000 males in Census 2001 and improved to 943 in 2011, when women comprised 48.5 per cent of the total population, and argues that a balanced sex ratio is vital for equitable development, social stability, and sustainable population growth. A student exploration activity asks why some states such as Kerala, Puducherry, and Tamil Nadu recorded ratios at or above 1,000 while the national figure remained well below.

India's Demographic Dividend — A Limited Window of Opportunity

This closing section explains the opportunity and the deadline attached to India's age structure. The large share of children and young adults visible in the 2020 pyramid produces a youth bulge — a large young population aged 16–30, common in countries at Stage 2 of demographic transition — with around 27 per cent of Indians under 15 and nearly 65 per cent in the working-age group of 15–59 years; a table of median ages places India at 28 against Japan 50, Italy 48, Germany 47, China 40, and the USA 39. The demographic dividend is defined as the economic growth potential of a nation resulting from a younger and productive age structure: young people work in factories, offices, and farms or start businesses, expanding economic activity, earning income and paying taxes (a compulsory contribution levied by the government on income, profits, or the price of goods and services) that governments use to build schools, hospitals, roads, and parks. The window is limited: growth in the young population is expected to decline beyond 2050, the number of children under five peaked in 2007 and those under 15 peaked in 2011 and are now falling, so after 2050 India will begin transitioning towards an older population — a shift already visible in Kerala and Tamil Nadu compared with Bihar and Uttar Pradesh. An ageing population means more elderly needing care and fewer young workers to support them, pressure on healthcare systems, fewer workers to replace retirees, and difficulty paying pensions (regular state payments to people at or above official retirement age) from a smaller base of young taxpayers. The section then discusses preparation: developed countries must support an ageing society while maintaining productivity and innovation, as Japan has done by raising the retirement age and using technology, automation, community centres, and robot and AI assistants for elder care; developing countries must harness the dividend through investment in education, skilling, and healthcare, while addressing the 'double burden' faced by women managing both jobs and household duties, since a lack of childcare facilities, joint family support, and flexible working hours limits women's workforce participation — supportive family systems and daycare infrastructure can turn the dividend into lasting progress, and attempts by many nations to revive fertility rates have so far had little success. A "Think About It" activity links this to āshrama dharma, the Indian division of life into brahmacharya, gṛihastāshrama, vānaprasta, and sanyāsa, and asks students to consider the continuing contributions of older people to family and society.

Before we move on ...

The closing recap consolidates the chapter's argument in three points: demography is the study of population size, structure, and change through births, deaths, and migration in a region; a country's population changes over time in patterns called demographic transition, moving from high birth and death rates to low birth and death rates, with many developed countries now experiencing shrinking and ageing populations because of low birth rates; and India currently has a large young workforce, its demographic dividend, which can boost growth through the development of human capital in education, employment, and healthcare — but the window of opportunity is limited, as India's population will begin to fall in the coming decades.
