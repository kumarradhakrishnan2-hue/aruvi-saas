**Unit 9 in full — read this cold**

**Teacher notes.** This unit draws on all the concepts children have built — shape names and properties, square corners, 3D-to-2D faces, composition and decomposition — and invites them to connect those ideas across contexts they met at different points. The opening recall wall is the shared foundation: whatever route children took to learn the shapes, the properties they can articulate now are the common ground. Watch for children who still conflate square and rectangle; the sorting task surfaces this and gives a natural moment to revisit the all-sides-equal distinction.
- `0-8` Shape recall wall: children call out the name of every 2D shape studied — circle, triangle, rectangle, square — and for each one, a volunteer states one property (number of sides, corners, or something special about the sides). Scribe these on the board as a class-built reference.
- `8-17` Property sorting: display or draw six mixed shapes on the board (some squares, some rectangles, some triangles, some circles, one or two irregular polygons). Children work in pairs: sort shapes by a property of their choice — straight sides only, all sides equal, has a square corner, no corners — then share their sorting rule with another pair.
- `17-27` 3D-to-2D connection: hold up a cuboid object. Ask children to name the 2D face shapes they expect to find, recall how they discovered that cuboid faces are rectangles, and identify one real object in the room whose face would be a square rather than a rectangle. Children record their answers in writing.
- `27-37` Create and compare: each child draws a design of their own — a rangoli, a simple picture, or a border pattern — using at least three of the four main shapes. They then label each shape in their design and write one sentence describing how two of the shapes are the same and one way they are different.
- `37-40` Closing reflection: children share one shape or property they found surprising or interesting during the chapter — spoken round, no writing required. The teacher accepts all responses and points back to the concrete experiences (rangoli, boxes, matchsticks, circle folding) that surfaced each idea.
